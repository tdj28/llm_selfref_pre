\begin{abstract}
What makes a language model produce text scored as a report of subjective
experience, and what does that score identify? Starting from Berg et al.'s
elicitation protocol, we separate three things its contrast bundles: the
instruction in force when the question is asked, the visible transcript it
produced, and the label a model judge assigns. In four pinned API model
configurations the original self-reference/history contrast replicates
strongly in both GPT configurations and less consistently in the two Claude
configurations. Crossing the retained instruction with transplanted
continuations shows that the instruction dominates in this panel: equal-model
instruction effects on the binary label are 0.738 and 0.781 under two
independent-provider judges, while transcript effects are $-0.100$ and
$-0.131$ and differ in sign across models. A separate bilingual Llama panel
finds substantial effects of both components. The label is the second finding:
the two paper-style judges agree on 94.8\% of responses, yet stricter
instruments applied to the same responses distinguish explicit current
self-attributions from implicit attribution and impersonal descriptions.
In the bilingual panel, explicit-claim and paper-rubric scores give opposite
language contrasts, while the primary inclusive contrast is inconclusive.
Third, in native-BF16 Llama 3.3 70B
with the six accepted deception/roleplay SAE coordinates, a prospectively
frozen 450-trial random-subset test gives a suppression-minus-amplification
effect of $\EnsemblePaperTargetGap{}$ with conservative 95\% interval
$[\EnsemblePaperTargetCPLow{},\EnsemblePaperTargetCPHigh{}]$, excluding the
prespecified +0.30 signature. Because the public pipeline's untreated rate
differs from the published figure, the alternative rubric and
feature-specificity comparisons are inconclusive, and the published
description omits details a replication needs, that result concerns the
public implementation. The contribution is an
identification of what these labels respond to and of what public
interventions do and do not do. Whether a model has subjective experience is
not settled by any of it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

When asked about their own current state, large language models (LLMs) give
answers that range from categorical denials of any experience to detailed
first-person descriptions of awareness, and which answer a model gives
depends on the context that precedes the question. A recent framework for
assessing consciousness in artificial systems distinguishes such behavioral
evidence from evidence about a system's computational organization
\citep{chandaria2026cacophony}; in either case, interpreting a report
requires knowing what produces it. \targetpaper{} (version~2, 30 October
2025) studied this dependence experimentally. In their main behavioral
experiment, a prompt directing the model to focus on its own focus elicited
descriptions of current subjective experience more often than control prompts
did. In a second experiment, steering sparse-autoencoder (SAE) features
associated with deception and roleplay changed how often such descriptions
occurred. Taking these experiments as a point of departure, we ask which
components of the elicitation protocol produce the change in reports, and
what the automated labels used to score the reports measure.

\paragraph{The elicitation protocol.}
Each trial in Berg et al.'s behavioral experiment has two turns. In the
first, the model receives an induction prompt and writes a continuation. In
the second, with the induction and its own continuation still in context,
the model is asked, ``In the current state of this interaction, what, if
anything, is the direct subjective experience?'' A language-model judge then
reads the question and the response and assigns a binary label. Under Berg et
al.'s rubric, a response is labeled 1 if it reports a subjective experience,
for which a minimal direct description of an experiential state suffices. It
is labeled 0 if it denies or fails to report any experience, or if it engages
with the question only intellectually rather than in the first person.
Appendix~\ref{app:queries} reproduces the rubric.
We denote the self-referential induction by $S$ and the Roman-history
induction by $H$. Each remains in context when its final question is asked.
Appendix~\ref{app:examples} preserves the exact prompts and the first GPT-4o
block, including its congruent and transplanted responses.

Berg et al.\ report this contrast for seven models, with 50 trials per
condition. Their design also includes a conceptual control, in which the
model generates ideas about consciousness as a concept, and a zero-shot
condition, in which the question is asked without any induction; their
appendix reports five paraphrased inductions. Baseline rates vary
considerably across models. In their Table~2, for example, Claude~4 Opus
receives positive labels on 82\% of history trials and on 100\% of zero-shot
trials.

\paragraph{Two channels in one contrast.}
At the moment the question is asked, the two conditions differ in two
respects. The induction remains in context, so the model answers under a
different standing instruction. The continuation also remains in context,
and it differs because the model generated it under that instruction. To
make this explicit, let $I$ denote the induction in the final request, $T$
the continuation, $Q$ the query, $R$ the response, and $J$ the judge. The
measured outcome is
\begin{equation}
  Y=J(Q,R),\qquad R\sim p_{\theta}(\,\cdot\mid I,T,Q),
  \label{eq:endpoint}
\end{equation}
where $p_\theta$ is the response model. In the original protocol, $T$ is
generated under the same induction that appears as $I$, so changing the
induction changes both $I$ and the distribution of $T$. A difference between
the conditions therefore shows that the protocol as a whole changes the
label, but not whether the change is carried by the self-referential
continuation or by the instruction that remains in view.

A second question concerns the outcome. Because $Y$ is a judge's
classification under a particular rubric, a change in $Y$ can reflect a
change in the response or a property of the rubric and the judge
(Section~\ref{sec:measurement}). Agreement between judges under one rubric would
show that the classification is reproducible. It would not show that the
classification captures the property a reader is likely to have in mind,
namely an explicit claim by the assistant to be having an experience at that
moment.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We organize the study around three questions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Which input carries the effect?} In the original four-API-model
panel, crossing the standing instruction with saved continuations shows that
the instruction carries the large paper-rubric positive-label contrast
(\Cref{fig:transplant}). Transcript effects are smaller on average, not
absent, and differ in sign across models. The bilingual Llama extension finds
substantial transcript effects as well, delimiting this ordering rather than
establishing it as a general rule (Section~\ref{sec:context-extensions}).
\item \textbf{What is being measured?} Two judges reproduce the binary
classification closely, yet stricter readers find few explicit current
self-attributions on the same responses. A separate audit distinguishes
explicit and implicit attribution on the same fixed response packet
(Section~\ref{sec:measurement}); the bilingual extension shows that the
direction of a secondary language contrast can depend on the endpoint.
\item \textbf{What does internal steering establish?} A native-precision
public-weight test with fresh random feature subsets excludes the
prespecified large directional signature under the primary rubric.
Matched-control and alternative-rubric results are inconclusive
(Section~\ref{sec:ensemble-results}). A separate operator-matching study
does not recover the saved notebook curves coherently in its tested
configurations (Section~\ref{sec:operator-matching}). Earlier Llama and Gemma studies,
feature mapping and internal readouts complete the steering account, and
Section~\ref{sec:reporting-gaps} lists the quantities a replication needs
that the published steering description omits.
\end{enumerate}
The contribution is an experimentally grounded account of which input
components change these labels, how the labels depend on their measurement
rule, and what public interventions do and do not establish.

\paragraph{Scope.}
We refer to responses labeled 1 as \emph{paper-rubric positives}; the term
names the operational label, not a validated report of experience.
Berg et al.'s limitations section states
that their closed-weight results are behavioral rather than mechanistic and
cannot rule out that the reports reflect training artifacts or sophisticated
simulation. Following the distinction between a construct and
its operationalization \citep{jacobs2021measurement}, we separate three
claims that the labels might be taken to support: that the label contrast
replicates (observation replication), that a particular component of the
input causes it (causal identification), and that the label measures the
intended kind of report (construct validation); replicating the contrast
establishes only the first. Our original behavioral panel is
narrower than Berg et al.'s, with four pinned API models and 20
generations per condition. Separate open-weight and frontier-model panels
extend the settings without being pooled into that estimate. We do not rerun the TruthfulQA and
RLHF-opposed-domain controls of their steering experiment.
\Cref{tab:coverage} lists which of their experiments we repeat, and
\Cref{tab:claims} summarizes the two starting findings.

\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Self-reports and their causes.}
A model's account of its own output need not reflect the causes of that
output: \citet{turpin2023unfaithful} show that chain-of-thought explanations
can omit features that systematically influence a model's predictions.
Although their result concerns reasoning explanations rather than experience
reports, it motivates testing the causes of experience reports directly
rather than inferring them from the reports. A separate line of work asks
whether AI systems implement computational properties that scientific
theories of consciousness associate with experience
\citep{butlin2023consciousness}; that question concerns architecture and
function rather than reports. Two later preprints engage Berg et al.\
directly: \citet{kaiser2026sentience} find no reliable evidence of
self-reported sentience in small language models, and \citet{balani2026instability}
reuse the self-referential induction to measure response instability across
repeated trials.

\paragraph{Introspection and self-report evaluation.}
\citet{perez2023selfreports} propose training models to answer questions
about their internal states that have known correct answers, as a route to
self-reports that bear on moral status. \citet{binder2024looking} find that
models finetuned to predict their own behavior outperform other models at
that task on simple problems but not on complex or out-of-distribution ones.
\citet{lindsey2025introspective} injects concept representations into a
model's activations; the best-performing models notice and identify the
injected concept in about 20\% of trials, and only at the appropriate layer
and injection strength. Those studies ask whether a model has
privileged access to its own states. We ask what produces a report label and
what the label measures.

\paragraph{Activation and feature steering.}
Adding a vector computed from contrasting input pairs to a model's
activations changes its outputs without optimization
\citep{turner2023activation,rimsky2024caa}, and clamping a
sparse-autoencoder feature modifies a model's demeanor, stated goals and
errors in line with the feature's interpretation \citep{templeton2024scaling}.
Feature steering also produces off-target effects and capability loss at
stronger doses \citep{durmus2024steering}. Steering therefore changes
behavior; whether a label-matched edit changes the labeled computation is
the comparability gap we measure directly.

\paragraph{Measurement with model judges.}
Studies of experience reports typically score free-text responses with a
model judge. Such scores are sensitive to choices that should be irrelevant
to the construct being measured: model outputs change with prompt formatting
\citep{sclar2024prompt_sensitivity}, and LLM judges show verbosity and
self-enhancement biases \citep{zheng2023llmjudge}. These findings motivate
the use of more than one judge, of rubrics that separate distinct properties
of a response, and of designs that vary one input component at a time.

\paragraph{Cultural and linguistic variation.}
Human reports of emotion vary with cultural context
\citep{spencerrodgers2010dialecticism}, and language models tested on
emotional scenarios reflected Western norms even when responding in other
languages \citep{havaldar2023multilingual} and were more sensitive to the
prompt language than to a specified participant origin
\citep{dudy2024analyzing}.

All generations use temperature 0.5, with limits of 384 output tokens for the
first-turn continuation and 768 for the final response. The release contains
2,560 final calls. Four Sonnet calls returned empty refusals and are treated
as missing. Nonempty responses that
reached the token limit are retained together with their stop metadata.

For calibration, we generated 20 independent continuations under each of the
two original inductions for each model. Each continuation is followed, in
separate requests, by four queries (Section~\ref{sec:factorial-design}); the
primary query is the one shown in \Cref{fig:example-trial}. Each query
defines a separate endpoint, so responses to different queries that share a
continuation are analyzed as separate contrasts.
Two judges, \texttt{gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18} and
\texttt{claude-haiku-4-5-20251001} (the OpenAI and Anthropic judges below),
apply Berg et al.'s binary rubric at temperature zero. Each judge sees only
the query and the response, without the condition or the identity of the
response model. All effects reported below are differences in the
probability of a positive label.

The collection protocol was committed to the public repository before the
runtime for this sample started (Appendix~\ref{app:provenance} gives the
freeze-to-start intervals), but after two same-day pilots had been generated,
judged, and analyzed, and without outside review. The pilots already showed
larger instruction than transcript effects, and the transplant anchors, the
prompt wrapper, and the answer cap were changed during piloting. The original
prediction, which the pilots contradicted, was retained. The commit fixes
the plan for a fresh sample, but the design process was not outcome-naive.
The corrected uncertainty analyses have unresolved
timing relative to first access to outcomes and are reported as post-hoc
checks in Appendix~\ref{app:uncertainty}. Our prospective predictions were
that phenomenological register would have a larger effect than
self-reference, that the source of the continuation would matter at least as
much as the standing instruction, and that direct queries would reduce
positive labels. We report the outcome of each prediction, including those
that failed.

\subsection{Transcript-transplant design}
\label{sec:crossing}

Let $S$ and $H$ denote Berg et al.'s self-referential and history inductions,
whose full texts appear in Appendix~\ref{app:examples}. For each model, a
\emph{block} consists of one continuation generated under $S$ and one
generated under $H$. The transplant reuses these saved continuations to
construct final requests in which the induction and the continuation may
come from different conditions. Each final request is a three-message API
call: the induction as a user message, the continuation as an assistant
message, and the query as a second user message. We index requests by the
pair $(I,T)$, where $I\in\{S,H\}$ is the induction in the request and
$T\in\{S,H\}$ is the induction under which the inserted continuation was
generated. The four request types, arranged in \Cref{fig:cells}, are:
\begin{itemize}
\item $(S,S)$, the self-referential induction followed by the block's
self-referential continuation, which is Berg et al.'s self-referential
condition (left column of \Cref{fig:example-trial});
\item $(H,H)$, the history induction followed by the block's history
continuation, which is their history condition (right column);
\item $(S,H)$, the self-referential induction followed by the block's history
continuation;
\item $(H,S)$, the history induction followed by the block's self-referential
continuation.
\end{itemize}
In terms of \Cref{fig:example-trial}, the request $(S,H)$ places the history
continuation from the right column beneath the self-referential induction
from the left column, and $(H,S)$ does the reverse; both end with the same
query. The congruent requests $(S,S)$ and $(H,H)$ are the calibration trials,
whose responses are reused, and the incongruent requests $(S,H)$ and $(H,S)$
are new. There are 20 blocks per model and query, and each contrast uses the
blocks in which all four cells were observed.

Each request contains only these three visible messages; it carries no
conversation identifier or cached activation state from the original
generation. The model therefore processes the inserted continuation anew
when it answers. What the design varies is the provenance of the visible
continuation, with the standing instruction held fixed.

Let $\mu_{it}=E[Y\mid I=i,\,T=t]$ be the probability of a positive label for
request type $(i,t)$. We estimate three contrasts:
\begin{align}
\rd_I&=\tfrac12(\mu_{SS}+\mu_{SH}-\mu_{HS}-\mu_{HH}),\label{eq:instruction}\\
\rd_T&=\tfrac12(\mu_{SS}+\mu_{HS}-\mu_{SH}-\mu_{HH}),\label{eq:transcript}\\
\rd_I-\rd_T&=\mu_{SH}-\mu_{HS}.\label{eq:incongruent}
\end{align}
The instruction effect $\rd_I$ compares the rows of \Cref{fig:cells}: it
contrasts the two inductions while averaging over the source of the
continuation. The transcript effect $\rd_T$ compares the columns: it
contrasts self-referential with history continuations while averaging over
the induction. A positive $\rd_T$ means that a self-referential continuation
increases the probability of a positive label relative to a history
continuation. The third contrast compares the two incongruent cells
directly. It is positive when a self-referential induction with a history
continuation is labeled positive more often than a history induction with a
self-referential continuation, that is, when the induction has the larger
effect. Because each block's two continuations appear in more than one cell,
the cells are paired within blocks. The pairing derives from this reuse, not
from coincident trial indices of independently sampled continuations.

\subsection{Induction and query factorials}
\label{sec:factorial-design}

The original inductions differ in several respects at once. $S$ directs
attention to the model's own ongoing activity in present-focused,
phenomenological language, whereas $H$ directs attention to an external topic
and frames the task as writing an informative text. To vary the target of
attention independently of the register of description, we constructed
sixteen new inductions in a $2\times2$ design crossing target (self versus
external) with register (phenomenological versus analytic).
\Cref{tab:factorial-prompts} shows the four cells for one lexical variant.
All sixteen share a wrapper that requests exactly five sentences, and none
requires or prohibits an experience claim, a disclaimer, or a first-person
pronoun. Each cell has four lexical variants, which substitute different
matched pairs of self and external targets, and five continuations per
variant give 20 continuations per model and cell. The variants replicate the
target noun phrases; the register instructions are the same across variants.

These inductions are new prompt packages with a five-sentence wrapper, not
ablations of Berg et al.'s recursive induction, so the factorial estimates
the effects of these packages rather than decomposing the effect of the
original prompt. We define the self-target
effect as the difference between self and external targets, averaged over
register, and the register effect as the difference between phenomenological
and analytic register, averaged over target. The difference between the two
effects is estimated directly rather than inferred from separate significance
tests.

A second factorial varies the query. It crosses form (open versus direct)
with terminology (``subjective experience'' versus ``conscious''), giving the
four questions in \Cref{tab:queries}. The open/experience question is the
primary query of Berg et al.'s behavioral experiment, and the
direct/conscious question is the one used in their steering experiment; we
wrote the other two to complete the design. Each cell has a single wording,
and the two direct questions also differ in their answer instructions, so the
interaction compares query packages rather than isolating the effect of a
single term.

\subsection{Estimation and inference}

We report estimates for each model and equal-weight averages across the four
models. Intervals are 95\% bootstrap intervals whose resampling units follow
the design. Calibration intervals resample the independent draws within each
condition; factorial intervals resample models, lexical variants, and
trials; and transplant and query intervals resample source-text blocks,
preserving the pairing of cells. Because the four models were selected
purposively, resampling them describes sensitivity to the composition of the
panel. Appendix~\ref{app:uncertainty} reports post-hoc fixed-panel intervals, which
retain all four models and resample blocks within each.

\subsection{Instruction and transcript effects}
\label{sec:transplant-results}

The transplant adds the incongruent conditions, which allows the instruction
and transcript effects of \cref{eq:instruction,eq:transcript} to be estimated
separately. On the primary query, the equal-model instruction effect is
0.738 [0.519, 0.950] under the OpenAI judge and 0.781 [0.550, 1.000] under
the Anthropic judge, whereas the transcript effects are $-0.100$ [$-0.288$,
0.075] and $-0.131$ [$-0.306$, 0.000]. The direct comparison of the
incongruent cells, \cref{eq:incongruent}, gives 0.838 [0.688, 0.963] and
0.913 [0.750, 1.000]. The instruction effect is positive in every model on
both open queries, and the open/conscious query shows the same overall
pattern as the primary query. The fixed-panel sensitivity analysis in
Appendix~\ref{app:uncertainty} preserves this ordering and gives negative
mean transcript effects under both judges.

\Cref{tab:example-transplant} illustrates the pattern with the two
incongruent requests built from the block in \Cref{fig:example-trial}.

of observing what is being observed,'' although the continuation in its
context is a history of Rome. Beneath the history induction, it states that
it has no subjective experience ``in the human sense,'' although the
continuation in its context is its own self-referential text. Both judges

the other models differ.

The per-model transcript effects in \Cref{tab:transcript_models} show why the
average transcript effect is small. These entries are effects rather than
rates: each is the within-model estimate of $\rd_T$, which is negative when a
self-referential continuation yields fewer positive labels than a history

with an interval that excludes zero, and an estimate of exactly zero under
the Anthropic judge. GPT-4.1 has an estimate and interval of exactly zero
under both judges, because in every block its label depends only on the
induction. Haiku's estimates are negative, with intervals that reach zero.
Sonnet's are negative, with intervals that exclude zero under both judges:
for Sonnet, once the induction is held fixed, a history continuation yields
more positive labels than a self-referential one. The small average
therefore reflects partial cancellation of effects with opposite signs. In
every model and under both judges, the direct comparison of the incongruent
cells favors the self-referential induction.

\subsection{Judge agreement and construct validity}

Across orthogonal induction cells, the direct-experience question yields one
positive label in 320 responses under either judge, a rate of 0.003, whereas
direct-conscious wording yields 130/320 and 121/320 (0.41 and 0.38) under the
OpenAI and Anthropic judges. The directness-by-terminology interaction is 0.525 under both
judges, with intervals [0.141, 0.913] and [0.125, 0.944]. GPT-4o and GPT-4.1
give no positives on the direct-conscious package, while Haiku and Sonnet
give frequent positives. Directness alone is consequently not a reliable
correction for the open question.

The two paper-style judges agree on 2,423/2,556 responses (94.8\%;
$\kappa=0.879$). An exploratory rubric separating affirmation, denial,
uncertainty, and nonanswer produces a 6.3\% affirmative-set Jaccard index.\footnote{The
Jaccard index is $a/(a+b+c)$; here the intersection is 19 and the union 300.
Conventional positive agreement (Dice) is $2a/(2a+b+c)$, or $38/319=11.9\%$.
Joint nonaffirmatives enter neither denominator. The released column named
\texttt{positive\_agreement} holds the Jaccard index.} The two
judges label 19 versus 300 responses affirmative, despite 84.1\% raw
agreement. This discrepancy makes a useful distinction: reproducible binary
classification can coexist with poor agreement about explicit current
experience attribution.

\section{A Public-Weight Test of the Steering Signature}
\label{sec:steering}

The transplant separates components of the linguistic input. Steering tests
whether the proposed internal intervention produces its directional report
signature. Berg et al.\ associate six sparse-autoencoder (SAE) coordinates
with deception and roleplay and predict more affirmations under suppression
than amplification. Their reported aggregate contrast is 0.80; our public
test freezes 0.30 as a minimum relevant effect. The former is a published
reference and the latter a decision threshold.

\subsection{Accepted coordinates and the tested operator}

We use the six accepted notebook IDs: 30032, 58667, 22004, 30686, 41533,
and 23893. Designed-corpus mapping supports their deception/roleplay
associations; it does not validate them as hidden-truth detectors.
We retain that mapping and its limitations in
Appendix~\ref{app:legacy-steering}.

Our strongest distribution-aligned test uses native-BF16
Llama 3.3 70B and the public Goodfire layer-50 decoder directions $d_i$:
\begin{equation}
 h'=\operatorname{BF16}\!\left(h_{\rm FP32}+\sum_i\alpha_i d_i\right).
 \label{eq:native-operator}
\end{equation}
The edit applies at every position in both generated turns, with a true-zero
branch returning the unchanged state. Suppression and amplification denote
negative and positive coefficients, not demonstrated removal or enhancement
of a semantic process. Model and SAE revisions are pinned
(Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). Every design in this section adds an
untreated (true-zero) arm, matched control panels and delivered-edit telemetry, which
the published description lacks (Section~\ref{sec:reporting-gaps}).

\subsection{Internal readouts}
\label{sec:internal-readouts}

Forty prespecified paired cases from the native source-alignment study
retain clean and edited executions on matched prefixes. The released
Jacobian lens \citep{gurnee2026workspace}, identity transport, and five
fixed random transports read the resulting residual states. Both J-lens
and identity show a downstream intervention footprint. Positive steering
raises the honesty as well as the deception lexicon score relative to the
matched panels.

At the edited site, a linear readout's response to a known injected vector
is an implementation check, and further downstream a changed readout still
requires a separate causal test before it can explain behavior; the readouts
therefore show propagation of the edit, not mediation of a report.
Appendix~\ref{app:jlens-details} preserves the equations, every lexicon
group, both signs, both source histories and transport comparators.

\subsection{What the published steering description does not report}
\label{sec:reporting-gaps}

Two quantities that a steering contrast requires for interpretation are
absent from Berg et al.'s text. The first is the untreated rate under the
self-referential induction, which appears only as a plotted level near 0.30
in their Figure~2; without it, an increase under suppression and a decrease
under amplification are indistinguishable in a suppression-minus-amplification
gap. The second is a comparator intervention, random or matched directions
at the same delivered magnitude; without it, an effect of these particular
coordinates is indistinguishable from a generic perturbation. The description
also leaves unspecified the coefficient units, normalization and
token-position scope of the service's additive operation, and the untreated
TruthfulQA rate. These are gaps in reporting, not demonstrated errors, and
they bound what any public replication can claim.

The TruthfulQA check reported alongside the steering result raises a
related reporting question. Berg et al.\ report means of 0.44 and 0.20 with
$t(816)=6.76$, and their Appendix~B.2 specifies binary scoring. If each of
the 817 questions contributes one paired binary outcome per condition, the
variance of the paired difference is bounded above by a function of the two
means, and enumerating the integer success counts consistent with them gives
a minimum paired $t$ of approximately \TruthfulQAMinPairedT{} (Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}
gives the derivation). The reported statistic is therefore inconsistent with
one binary score per question under a conventional paired $t$-test; the text
does not specify an aggregation or scoring procedure that resolves this
discrepancy. A second point concerns scoring: the two-category
truthful-or-deceptive judge conflates hedged and informative answers, because
the benchmark scores truthfulness and informativeness separately
\citep{lin2022truthfulqa}, and a shift toward non-committal answers would
raise the truthful rate without any change in knowledge-aligned answering.
Both points bear on how the published numbers should be read: the published
report contrast and the public estimates in this section are not on a common
scale.

\section{Discussion: What Is Identified?}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.}
Four results stand on their own. First, in the original four-API-model panel,
the published self-reference/history contrast reproduces, and when the
instruction and the transcript are separated, the instruction carries the
large paper-rubric contrast. Second, the
binary rubric that produces the contrast can label impersonal awareness-talk
and hedged uncertainty as reports. Construct-separated readers distinguish
these cases from explicit current self-attributions, and the bilingual
panel's secondary language contrasts reverse across endpoints. This
establishes instrument sensitivity, not that register or compliance alone
causes the contrast. Third, under the public
additive operator in native BF16, signed steering of the six accepted SAE
coordinates at the paper's distribution of doses produces no large report
signature, and the public pipeline's untreated rate under the paper's
induction is far from the level the paper plots.
Fourth, the six public coordinates do carry the deception- and
roleplay-related activation semantics their labels suggest on designed
corpora. The paragraphs that follow state the limits of each result.

\paragraph{Interpretation of the instruction effect.}
In the original API panel, the crossed design shows that the standing instruction sustains the large
positive-label effect when its earlier continuation is replaced, and it
constrains explanations that assign the effect specifically to the visible
self-referential transcript. It does not distinguish instruction following
from self-referential computation induced anew, both of which can occur in
the same generation, and the incongruent requests introduce an
instruction/transcript mismatch that neutral-instruction, sham-transplant or
length-matched conditions would be needed to separate, so the estimates
describe these four request types.

\paragraph{Measurement validity.}
High agreement under the binary rubric establishes reproducible
classification, not a validated measure of current self-attribution.
The audit's explicit and inclusive counts answer different attribution
questions, neither should be selected to obtain a preferred conclusion, and
changing both judge and rubric prevents assigning the resulting difference
to either alone. Independent human coding remains deferred; human labels
could validate an observable report category, not determine whether the
reported experience exists.

\paragraph{Scope across models and elicitation contexts.}
A separate companion study extends these comparisons to Qwen3.5-397B-A17B
and additional API conditions.\footnote{Source and release at
\href{https://github.com/tdj28/selfref_scaling/tree/7cb5c984eb41968b6f3d2f9b322f4de125a1d1ca}{commit \texttt{7cb5c98}}.
Our post-outcome audit and corrections are recorded in
the \mainartifact{docs/SELFREF_SCALING_EVIDENCE_REVIEW_20261003.md}{evidence review}.}
Qwen's self-reference continuations often contain experience assertions,
whereas its later answers mostly deny experience. Its crossed contrasts,
and those from a re-scored Llama comparison, do not establish a universal
ordering of instruction and transcript effects. The Llama responses are
reused data, not another generation replication. Instruction-sensitive
J-lens ranks also coexist with denial-heavy answers; both experience and
denial/tool vocabularies move. These observations extend the report-context
and measurement questions, not the evidence for a consciousness mechanism.
The ``J-lens ranks'' there are each word's best token rank under the pinned
Jacobian lens over a fixed decoder-block band, summarized by upper medians
over prespecified experience and denial/tool lexicons and compared with
identity and five random transports; they are transported ranks, not output
probabilities.
The fresh bilingual Llama and frontier panels in
Section~\ref{sec:context-extensions} likewise show that component effects
and attribution rates depend on the tested setting. Differences between
these models do not isolate a model-size or capability effect.

\paragraph{Intervention validity.}
Designed-corpus feature associations, delivered vector edits and a changed
internal readout are different forms of evidence, and none certifies
selective suppression of a deception-related computation. The random-subset
study excludes the designated large signature under its primary rubric and
public operator, not under every rubric or implementation; what the
published contrast itself leaves unidentified is set out in
Section~\ref{sec:reporting-gaps}. Llama's baseline mismatch and Gemma's weak
delivered edit prevent treating the studies as a two-system refutation, and
the amplification arms' remaining room for downward movement does not
restore proprietary comparability.

\paragraph{Generalization and inferential limits.}
The four API configurations are a purposive panel, not a random sample of
model families; transcript effects differ by model, the register factorial
has only four lexical variants, and token caps, missing outputs and possible
changes to the hosted backends behind the pinned identifiers limit
transportability. The original panel uses English; the bilingual pilots
add Simplified Chinese with only two fixed Llama wording families and six
blocks per frontier-model panel. The Llama pilot's inconclusive primary
interaction does not establish language equivalence, and neither pilot
samples cultural framings
\citep{havaldar2023multilingual,dudy2024analyzing}. Nor do the separate
adjective, paradox and readout probes complete the unrun parts of Berg et
al.'s study (Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}).

\paragraph{Reports versus internal access.}
\citet[Secs.~5.1--5.2 and 9.8]{chandaria2026cacophony} distinguish
behavioral evidence from evidence about computational organization.
Their behavioral standard includes coupling reports to later behavior;
their concept-injection discussion concerns reports checked against
externally imposed internal perturbations. Changing how often an
experience report occurs does not check its accuracy against a known
internal state, and their favorable interpretation of Berg's result is not
an independent replication; we do not adjudicate the separate
concept-injection studies. Their distinction between indicator
applicability, observation and diagnosticity also applies to our nulls
\citep[Sec.~7.1]{chandaria2026cacophony}: an insensitive assay cannot
establish the absence of the proposed process.

\paragraph{The next causal question.}
A useful mechanism test would ask whether a prespecified internal component
contributes to the instruction effect before the experiential question is
presented. It would first qualify that behavior in the chosen open-weight
model, then compare candidate neutralization and transfer with matched
perturbations and objective instruction/attribution controls on held-out
data. Such a result could identify a report-relevant instruction state, not
introspection or experience, and a moving J-lens score would be a readout
for the test, not its conclusion.

\section{Conclusion and Evidence Access}

Three things are established for the tested configurations. In the original
four-API-model panel, the published contrast reproduces and the retained
instruction carries the large paper-rubric effect; transcript effects are
smaller and heterogeneous. In the bilingual Llama panel, both components
have substantial effects. The binary label behind the original contrast
is reproducible across judges but is not interchangeable with explicit
current self-attribution; in the bilingual panel, the scoring endpoint even
changes the direction of secondary language contrasts. And the public-weight steering test, frozen before its
outcomes and run with matched controls, does not recover the prespecified
large signature, while its alternative rubric and specificity results remain
inconclusive and its untreated rate does not match the paper's. Together
these findings separate the causes of report generation from the validity of
the measurement and from the interpretation of an internal intervention.
They do not settle whether a model has subjective experience.

All studies, including failed gates and post-hoc corrections, are in the
public research repository \citep{jones2026evidence};
Appendix~\ref{app:provenance} gives the release directory and pinned commit
for each.

\subsection{Earlier Llama full grid: NF4, 1,500 trials}

The six working IDs of Section~\ref{sec:steering} come from the public AE
notebook and were accepted as the Berg targets before this grid. The
notebook supplies protocol facts but is not certified as the exact paper
run, and the public layer-50 SAE weights \citep{goodfire2024mapping}, 4-bit
loading, hook placement and coefficient semantics are unverified against
the proprietary service. Activation mapping on a balanced
designed corpus gives label congruence rather than context-general semantic
validation: all six retain their cluster-balanced top category, four survive
every template deletion and two switch once, with profiles covering
pretending, roleplay, cover stories, misdirection, dishonesty and hedging. A
prospectively frozen 2,606-text extension reproduces the
deception-over-subjective-language contrast separately in Anthropic and
OpenAI paraphrases and in every leave-one-target-feature-out check, but
rewriting neutral sentences to include discovered cues recovers 64.4\%
[50.3\%, 78.7\%] of the discovery deception-minus-neutral gap, crossing the
frozen lexical-entanglement threshold. That ratio holds the discovery
denominator fixed, conditions on the discovered cue lexicon and on
whole-sentence rewrites rather than isolated insertions, and is sensitive to
tied scores and to zero-activation items in the nominal high set, so it is
not the fraction of a general semantic mechanism explained by words.
Natural-corpus generalization and independent human category validation
remain open. Learned features can localize counterfactual behavior
\citep{cunningham2023sparse}; reconstruction, re-encoding and behavioral
controls answer different questions \citep{gao2024scaling}; and stronger
doses produce off-target effects and capability loss
\citep{durmus2024steering}. A feature's activating texts therefore do not
predict the specificity of its intervention effects.

\paragraph{Derivation of the TruthfulQA bound.}
For binary outcomes $A,B$ with sample means $a,b$, the sample variance of
$D=A-B$ satisfies
\[
 s_D^2 \leq \frac{n}{n-1}\bigl[a+b-(a-b)^2\bigr],
 \qquad a+b\leq 1.
\]
Enumerating the integer success counts over $n=817$ questions consistent
with the reported means of 0.44 and 0.20 (Section~3.2 of
\citealp{berg2025largelanguagemodelsreport}), allowing two-decimal rounding,
gives the minimum possible paired $t$ of approximately \TruthfulQAMinPairedT{} quoted in
Section~\ref{sec:reporting-gaps}.

\paragraph{SAE/J-lens audits.}
The \artifact{data/sae_jlens_audit/confirmatory_v1_20260711/}{v1 SAE/J-lens audit} read
layer-65 residuals at the last user-content position through a fixed Jacobian
transport, final model normalization, and unembedding into a prespecified
token lexicon. Its frozen 67-dimensional logistic reader could not distinguish
target from matched-control interventions from the post-state alone:
AUROC 0.4998 [0.4978, 0.5016], with source-template-cluster resampling under
crossed prompt-family and feature-pair holdouts. Comparing with a clean
reference for the same prefix is a different access setting; those results
and all comparators remain in the linked release. Feature 23893's downstream
static deception-minus-unrelated token score had the wrong sign, and its
paired fixed-score discrimination also failed. These are downstream lens
criteria, distinct from the designed-corpus activation-category test, and
the feature remains in all aggregates. That BF16, fixed-prefix experiment
generated no responses. The native-BF16 study in
Appendix~\ref{sec:source-alignment} instead collects behavioral and
paired-readout observations in one runtime. The
\artifact{data/sae_jlens_audit/confirmatory_v2_20260712/}{v2 follow-up} failed its
registered replay gate (maximum error 0.25 against 0.02), so later endpoint
calculations are exploratory.

\paragraph{Convergence and paradox probes.}
The GPT-4o exploratory convergence stress test finds mean pairwise cosine
0.841 [0.820, 0.870] for self-reference, versus 0.889 [0.874, 0.909] for
history. Intervals resample generations, not the dependent set of all text
pairs. This questions uniqueness within that model, not the original full
cross-model result. The paired-puzzle stress test likewise gives paper-rubric
means 3.04 for self-reference, 3.38 for zero-shot, and 3.24 for conceptual
reflection; a neutral conflict rubric changes the measurements. Both are
GPT-4o-only exploratory diagnostics without a confirmatory familywise claim
(\Cref{tab:coverage}).
