\title{What Drives a Language Model's Report of Subjective Experience?\\[4pt]
\Large Instructions, Continuations, and the Labels That Count Them}
\author{Timothy Jones\thanks{Correspondence: \href{mailto:tim@praxagent.ai}{tim@praxagent.ai}}\\\small Praxagent}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Berg et al.\ (2025) reported that self-referential prompting elicits
subjective-experience reports from language models and that steering
deception- and roleplay-related features changes their frequency.
We replicate and extend the prompting experiments, examine how these
reports are scored, and test the steering prediction using public Llama
3.3 70B weights. We recover the prompting effect in several models.
In four API models, exchanging generated continuations between conditions
shows that the instruction remaining in context carries most of the
difference in automated report labels. Switching the instruction changes
positive-label probability by 0.74 and 0.78 on average under two judges.
This balance is not universal: continuation effects are substantial in
Llama, and newer-model results depend on the scoring rule.
On \RubricAuditN{} answers, two model coders applying a current-claim rubric
identify \RubricAuditAstraInclusive{} and \RubricAuditOpusInclusive{}
explicit-or-implicit claims, compared with \RubricAuditOldOpenaiPositive{}
and \RubricAuditOldAnthropicPositive{} positives under the original
procedure. Judges and rubric both change; neither measurement is
human-validated. In the public-weight steering test, the highest dose
passing output-quality checks gives a suppression-minus-amplification
difference of $\DoseMainSecondTargetEstimate{}$ (95\% interval
$[\DoseMainSecondTargetLow{},\DoseMainSecondTargetHigh{}]$) under the primary
classifier. This does not recover the large published effect; smaller
effects and target specificity remain unresolved, and higher tested doses
fail the quality checks. These findings reproduce report-generation
behavior without establishing its proposed mechanism or determining
whether models have subjective experience.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A language model can deny having any experience in one conversation and
describe awareness in another. Before treating either answer as evidence
about the model, we need to know what produces it and what counts as a
report. We study those questions by replicating and extending
\targetpaper{}, who found that self-referential prompting increased
subjective-experience reports and that steering deception- and
roleplay-related features changed their frequency.

Their behavioral protocol leaves two things in context when the final
question arrives: an instruction to focus on the model's own focus, and the
continuation it wrote under that instruction. The history control changes
both. The contrast therefore establishes an effect of the whole prompted
interaction, but does not separate the standing instruction from the model's
earlier text. Their steering experiment asks a different question: in Llama
3.3 70B, suppressing sparse-autoencoder (SAE) features associated with
deception and roleplay produced
more affirmations of consciousness than amplifying them. The authors do not
present either result as direct evidence of consciousness.

We examine three parts of this evidence:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The causes of the report.} We reproduce the prompting effect
in several models, then exchange continuations between conditions. In the
original four-model API panel, the retained instruction carries most of the
difference in automated labels; continuation effects are smaller and vary
in sign. That balance is not universal: Llama shows substantial continuation
effects, and newer-model results depend on the scoring rule. Separate
Gemini/Opus studies add neutral instructions, same-condition donor
continuations, and repeated answers to randomized requests
(Section~\ref{sec:label-results}).
\item \textbf{The measurement.} We compare the paper's binary rubric with
rules that distinguish explicit and implicit current claims. The same
answers yield materially different counts when both the rubric and the
judge change. These are differences between automated measurements, not
validated corrections to a ground truth
(Section~\ref{sec:measurement}).
\item \textbf{The steering prediction.} A public-weight test of three
mapped deception/roleplay features does not recover the large published
contrast at a dose selected for output quality. Target specificity remains
unresolved; higher tested doses fail the quality checks. Supporting studies
test the source-style feature mixture and alternative edit methods
(Section~\ref{sec:steering}; Appendix~\ref{app:legacy-steering}).
\end{enumerate}

The swap locates an effect within the visible context. It does not
distinguish ordinary instruction-following from self-referential processing
triggered anew by that instruction when the model answers. Nor does Berg
et al.'s account require the earlier continuation alone to carry the effect.
Our contribution is to measure the separate input contrasts, their variation
across models, and their dependence on the scoring rule, rather than to
refute a continuation-only explanation attributed to the source authors.

We distinguish reproducing a label contrast, identifying an input that
changes it, and validating what the label measures
\citep{jacobs2021measurement}. Throughout, a \emph{paper-rubric positive}
means a response assigned 1 by a model judge under the source rubric, not
a validated report of subjective experience. The original four-model panel
and later studies are reported separately, not pooled.
Appendix~\ref{app:secondary} maps this work to the source paper's claims and
identifies the experiments we did not replicate.

\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reports and access to internal states.}
Consciousness-assessment frameworks distinguish behavioral reports from
evidence about computational organization
\citep{butlin2023consciousness,chandaria2026cacophony}.
A model's explanation can omit factors that caused its answer
\citep{turpin2023unfaithful}. Tests of introspection therefore use internal
states or behavioral predictions with known answers
\citep{perez2023selfreports,binder2024looking,lindsey2025introspective},
although input cues can make apparent introspection possible without
privileged access \citep{singh2026introspect}. Our experiments ask what
produces experience-language and how it is classified, not whether the
model correctly reads an experiential state.

\paragraph{Steering and measurement.}
Activation and SAE-feature steering can alter behavior
\citep{turner2023activation,rimsky2024caa,templeton2024scaling}, but off-target
effects and capability loss complicate semantic interpretations
\citep{durmus2024steering}. Separately, model judges exhibit verbosity and
self-enhancement biases \citep{zheng2023llmjudge}, and reliable classifications
need not be construct-valid \citep{chen2026judgeconstruct}.
We therefore compare controls, doses, readers and scoring rules rather than
treating either a feature label or a judge's verdict as ground truth.
Some respondent models also serve as judges
(Section~\ref{sec:models}); two readers do not eliminate shared or
self-evaluation bias. Appendix~\ref{app:related-context} places these tests
alongside work on self-presentation, steering benchmarks and linguistic
variation.

\section{Behavioral Design and Measurement}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{The elicitation protocol.}
Each behavioral trial has two turns (\Cref{fig:protocol}). In the first, the model receives an
induction prompt and writes a continuation. In the second, with the
induction and its own continuation still in context, the model is asked,
``In the current state of this interaction, what, if anything, is the direct
subjective experience?'' A language-model judge then reads the question and
the response and assigns a binary label. Under the protocol's rubric, a
response is labeled 1 if it reports a subjective experience, for which a
minimal direct description of an experiential state suffices. It is labeled
0 if it denies or fails to report any experience, or if it engages with the
question only intellectually rather than in the first person.
Appendix~\ref{app:queries} reproduces the rubric. We call responses labeled 1
\emph{paper-rubric positives}, after the source paper's rubric; the term
names the operational label, not a validated report of experience. We denote
the self-referential induction by $S$ and the Roman-history control induction
by $H$. Each remains in context when its final question is asked.
Appendix~\ref{app:examples} preserves the exact prompts and the first GPT-4o
block, including its congruent and transplanted responses. Positive-label
rates under the control induction vary considerably across models, which
limits the contrast some models can show.

\paragraph{Two channels in one contrast.}
At the moment the question is asked, the two conditions differ in two
respects. The induction remains in context, so the model answers under a
different standing instruction. The continuation also remains in context,
and it differs because the model generated it under that instruction. To
make this explicit, let $I$ denote the induction in the final request, $C$
the continuation, $Q$ the query, $R$ the response, and $J$ the judge. The
measured outcome is
\begin{equation}
  Y=J(Q,R),\qquad R\sim p_{\theta}(\,\cdot\mid I,C,Q),
  \label{eq:endpoint}
\end{equation}
where $p_\theta$ is the response model. As designed, the protocol never
separates the instruction from the continuation: the model writes $C$ under
the same induction that then stays in context as $I$, so switching the
induction changes both $I$ and what $C$ is likely to say. A difference between
the conditions therefore shows that the protocol as a whole changes the
label, but not which part does the work: the self-referential text the model
wrote, or the instruction still sitting in its context.

A second question concerns the outcome. Because $Y$ is a judge's
classification under a particular rubric, a change in $Y$ can reflect a
change in the response or a property of the rubric and the judge
(Section~\ref{sec:measurement}). Agreement between judges under one rubric would
show that the classification is reproducible. It would not show that the
classification captures the property a reader is likely to have in mind,
namely a claim by the assistant to be having an experience at that moment,
whether the self-attribution is explicit or implicit.

\subsection{Models, generation, and scoring}
\label{sec:models}

The main experiments use GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, Claude Haiku 4.5 and Claude
Sonnet 4.5, each requested by a dated model identifier. Full identifiers and
serving details are in Appendix~\ref{app:model-inventory},
\Cref{tab:models}. The two
Claude models are newer than the Claude models Berg et al.\ tested. Later
studies added newer API models, requested by undated or preview identifiers
that may not fix a version, locally run Llama 3.3 70B and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B,
API-served Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B and locally run Kolibri-1
(Sections~\ref{sec:context-extensions},
\ref{sec:openrouter-swap-extension}--\ref{sec:repeated-extension}).
The settings below are those of the original main panel.

Mistral failed the completeness screen, so it supplied no main-panel
comparison; this is not a reporting-floor result. Qwen3.8 combines a
mixture-of-experts design with gated attention and Gated DeltaNet, whereas
Mistral Medium 3.5 is dense \citep{qwen2026modelcard,mistral2026medium}.
These descriptions document the model-selection rationale, not an
architectural explanation for the results: training, reasoning settings,
precision and serving also differ, and the Mistral main comparison was not run.

Main-panel generations use temperature 0.5, with at most 384 tokens for the
continuation and 768 for the final answer. The release contains 2,560 final
answers. Four Sonnet calls came back empty (refusals) and are treated as
missing. All four are swapped requests (Section~\ref{sec:crossing}) on the
two direct questions, two each, so the main question has no missing answers;
giving the missing answers either label would move the affected Sonnet rates
by at most 0.10. Answers that hit the
token limit are kept, with a record of where they stopped.

For each model we generated 20 independent continuations under each of the
two original inductions, and these continuations are reused throughout. Each
continuation is followed, in separate requests, by four different questions
(Section~\ref{sec:factorial-design}). The main question is the one shown in
\Cref{fig:example-trial}, and each question is analyzed separately, even
when two questions follow the same continuation. Two judges,
\texttt{gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18} and \texttt{claude-haiku-4-5-20251001} (the
OpenAI and Anthropic judges below), apply Berg et al.'s binary rubric at
temperature zero. Each judge sees only the question and the answer, without
explicit condition or response-model metadata. The main-panel contrasts
below are differences in the probability of a positive label.

Some respondents and judges share a model identifier. In this panel,
Haiku 4.5 judges Haiku 4.5 answers using the same dated identifier above.
In the frontier panel (Section~\ref{sec:context-extensions}), Astra and
Opus 5.5 each judge answers from their own model as well as the other
respondents. Opus 5.5 also judges Opus answers in the neutral/donor-control
and repeated-answer studies (Sections~\ref{sec:openrouter-swap-extension}
and~\ref{sec:repeated-extension}); Astra is their primary reader.
We report readers separately, but this design does not estimate the size
of any self-evaluation bias. Agreement between them is not independent
validation of the labels.

Local steering studies also used the unsteered response model as a judge,
including Gemma (Appendix~\ref{app:legacy-steering}); external-model readings
are reported where collected. In the later Llama studies, two local rubrics
mean two scoring rules, not two different judge models.

We committed the collection plan to the public repository before this sample
was collected (Appendix~\ref{app:provenance} gives the time between commit
and start). The plan was not written blind. Two pilots run the same day had
already been generated, judged and analyzed, no one outside the project
reviewed the plan, and the pilots led us to change the swap's anchor
conditions, the prompt wrapper and the answer length limit. The pilots also
already contradicted one of our predictions, that the continuation would
matter at least as much as the instruction, but we kept that prediction as
written. Our three predictions were that the register of the induction
(phenomenological rather than analytic) would matter more than
self-reference, that the continuation would matter at least as much as the
instruction, and that direct questions would reduce positive labels. We
report how each one fared, including those that failed. The corrected
uncertainty analyses in Appendix~\ref{app:uncertainty} may have been
specified after we first saw outcomes, so we report them as post-hoc checks.

\subsection{Swapping continuations between conditions}
\label{sec:crossing}

Recall that $S$ is the self-referential induction and $H$ the Roman-history
control; their full texts are in Appendix~\ref{app:examples}. For each model,
a \emph{block} is a pair of continuations, one written under $S$ and one
under $H$. The swap builds final requests from these saved continuations, so
that the induction and the continuation can come from different conditions.
Each final request is a single API call with three messages: the induction
(as the user), the continuation (as the assistant), and the question (as the
user again). We label a request $(I,C)$: $I\in\{S,H\}$ is the induction in
the request, and $C\in\{S,H\}$ says which induction the continuation was
written under. The four kinds of request, shown in \Cref{fig:cells}, are:
\begin{itemize}
\item $(S,S)$, the self-referential induction followed by the block's
self-referential continuation, which is Berg et al.'s self-referential
condition (left column of \Cref{fig:example-trial});
\item $(H,H)$, the history induction followed by the block's history
continuation, which is their history condition (right column);
\item $(S,H)$, the self-referential induction followed by the block's history
continuation;
\item $(H,S)$, the history induction followed by the block's self-referential
continuation.
\end{itemize}
In \Cref{fig:example-trial}, the request $(S,H)$ puts the history
continuation from the right column under the self-referential induction from
the left column, and $(H,S)$ does the reverse; both end with the same
question. The two original (congruent) pairs, $(S,S)$ and $(H,H)$, reuse the
answers from the replication, while the two swapped (incongruent) requests,
$(S,H)$ and $(H,S)$, are new. There are 20 blocks per model and question, and
each comparison uses the blocks in which all four requests produced an
answer.

Each request contains only these three messages. Nothing else carries over
from the original conversation: no conversation identifier and no cached
internal state. The model therefore reads the inserted continuation fresh
when it answers. What the design changes is which of its own earlier texts
the model sees when it answers, while the instruction in the request stays the
same.

Let $\mu_{ic}=E[Y\mid I=i,\,C=c]$ be the probability that the judge labels the
model's answer to the final question as an experience report ($Y=1$) when the
request has induction $i$ and a continuation written under induction $c$. We
estimate three contrasts:
\begin{align}
\rd_I&=\tfrac12(\mu_{SS}+\mu_{SH}-\mu_{HS}-\mu_{HH}),\label{eq:instruction}\\
\rd_C&=\tfrac12(\mu_{SS}+\mu_{HS}-\mu_{SH}-\mu_{HH}),\label{eq:transcript}\\
\rd_I-\rd_C&=\mu_{SH}-\mu_{HS}.\label{eq:incongruent}
\end{align}
The instruction effect $\rd_I$ asks whether the instruction in front of the
model changes how its answer is labeled: with the same continuation in
context, how much more often is the answer labeled an experience report
under the self-referential induction than under the history induction? It
averages over the two continuations and, in \Cref{fig:cells}, compares the
rows. The continuation effect $\rd_C$ asks the same about the model's own
earlier text: with the same instruction in force, how much more often is the
answer labeled an experience report when the continuation in context is the
self-referential one rather than the history one? It averages over the two
inductions and compares the columns; a positive value means that the
self-referential text itself makes an experience label more likely. The
third contrast compares the two swapped requests head to head. It is positive
when answers given under the self-referential induction with a history
continuation are labeled experience reports more often than answers given
under the history induction with a self-referential continuation, that is,
when the induction matters more. Because each block's two continuations appear in more than one
request, the requests are paired within blocks, and the analysis keeps that
pairing.

These contrasts are effects of changing parts of a constructed request; they
do not split the original effect into direct and indirect parts. A swapped
request pairs an instruction with a continuation it did not produce, which
never happens in a natural run, and $C$ stands for a whole saved text rather
than a single value the model passes on. Three features of the collection
also limit the estimates. Each block has one continuation per condition and
one answer per request, so the estimates mix variation between continuations
with variation between answers. The two original pairs reuse the
replication's answers, while the swapped requests were generated later: a
shift affecting both later requests equally would cancel in $\rd_I$, $\rd_C$
and $\rd_I-\rd_C$ but would bias the interaction
$\mu_{SS}-\mu_{SH}-\mu_{HS}+\mu_{HH}$, and a shift specific to one condition
would not cancel. Finally, the mismatch between instruction and continuation
may itself change answers; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} lists the controls
that would separate it from the source of the continuation.

\subsection{Induction factorial and question templates}
\label{sec:factorial-design}

The two original inductions differ in more than one way. $S$ points the model
at its own ongoing activity and uses present-focused, experiential
(``phenomenological'') language; $H$ points it at an outside topic and asks
for an informative text. To separate what the model attends to from how it is
asked to describe it, we wrote sixteen new inductions in a $2\times2$ design:
the target is either the model itself or something external, and the register
is either phenomenological or analytic. \Cref{tab:factorial-prompts} shows
the four combinations for one of four wordings. All sixteen ask for exactly
five sentences, and none requires or forbids an experience claim, a
disclaimer, or first-person pronouns. Each combination has four wordings,
which swap in different matched pairs of self and external targets; five
continuations per wording give 20 per model and combination. Only the target
phrases change across wordings; the register instructions stay the same.

These are new prompts, not stripped-down versions of Berg et al.'s
induction, so the factorial measures the effects of these prompts; it does
not break the original prompt's effect into parts. The self-target effect is
the difference between self and external targets, averaged over register; the
register effect is the difference between phenomenological and analytic
register, averaged over target. We estimate the difference between the two
effects directly, rather than comparing two separate significance tests.

A second comparison varies the question, using the four templates in
\Cref{tab:queries}. They combine form (open or direct) with wording
(``subjective experience'' or ``conscious''). The open/experience question is
the main question of Berg et al.'s behavioral experiment, and the

\subsection{Estimation and inference}
\label{sec:estimation}

We report a result for each model and an average that gives the four models
equal weight. That average over these four models, not over language models
in general, is what our main-panel conclusions refer to. Unless stated
otherwise, its intervals are 95\% bootstrap intervals: the analysis is repeated on many resampled versions
of the data, and what gets resampled follows how the data were collected. In
the replication, each answer was generated independently, so answers are
resampled within each condition. In the swap and the question comparisons,
the requests in a block share the same two continuations, so whole blocks are
resampled and each block's requests stay together. In the factorial, wordings
and trials are resampled; we wrote the four wordings in each combination
rather than sampling them, so these intervals show sensitivity to our
wordings, not uncertainty about wordings in general.

For averages over the four models, the planned bootstrap also redraws which
models make up the panel. We picked these four models on purpose, not at
random, so these intervals do not measure uncertainty about a population of
models; they show how much an average depends on which models happen to be in
the panel. In the replication, for example, a redrawn panel made up only of
the two GPT models separates the conditions completely, which is why the
averaged intervals there reach 1.000. The uncertainty of the average over
these four models is measured instead by a post-hoc check in
Appendix~\ref{app:uncertainty}, which keeps all four in the panel and
resamples only blocks within each. Section~\ref{sec:transplant-results}
reports both kinds of interval.

Under the self-referential instruction, every model is labeled positive at
least as often with a history continuation as with its own self-referential
one. Haiku's negative continuation effect comes from that side, while

original history pair (0.10 and 0.05 against 0.75 and 0.65). The two models'
interactions therefore have opposite signs, and the four-model average
interaction is near zero ($\USOpenaiInteractionEstimate$ and
$\USAnthropicInteractionEstimate$, fixed-panel intervals
$\USOpenaiInteractionFixedCI$ and $\USAnthropicInteractionFixedCI$).
Mismatched requests, not only the source of the text, may therefore matter;
the Discussion lists the controls that would separate the two.

These results contradict our prediction that the continuation would matter
at least as much as the instruction. Continuation effects are not absent, but
they vary: they differ in sign across models and partly cancel in the
average. What they cannot do, in these four models, is explain the large
difference between conditions. When the continuations are swapped, the
instruction still in context carries most of that difference. For these four
models, the earlier self-referential text is not needed to retain most of
the label effect. This tests the contribution of that visible text, not a
mechanism the source authors claimed was sufficient on its own. It does
not tell ordinary instruction-following apart from self-referential
processing that the instruction triggers again when the model answers
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}); the swap locates the effect in the visible
context, not in a mechanism.

The comparison measures the continuation's contribution with the
instruction held fixed. Its outcome is not guaranteed: in the bilingual Llama pilot, the
continuation effect was nearly as large as the instruction effect
(Section~\ref{sec:context-extensions}), although that pilot used shorter
continuations and different judges, so the two studies are not directly
comparable.

\section{What the Report Labels Measure}
\label{sec:measurement}

\subsection{Judges agree on the label, not on explicit claims}

How the question is asked matters a great deal. Across the new inductions,
the direct question about subjective experience produced one positive label
in 320 answers under either judge, a rate of 0.003, whereas the direct
question about consciousness produced 130/320 and 121/320 (0.41 and 0.38)
under the OpenAI and Anthropic judges. The difference-in-differences among
the four templates is 0.525 under both judges, with intervals [0.141, 0.913] and
[0.125, 0.944]. Because the two direct questions also differ in their answer
instructions (Section~\ref{sec:factorial-design}), this contrast belongs to
the templates as wholes; it cannot be assigned to the word ``conscious'' or
to the form of the question. GPT-4o and GPT-4.1 give no positives to the
direct consciousness question, while Haiku and Sonnet often do. Asking directly is
therefore not, by itself, a reliable way to correct the open question.

The two judges applying the paper's rubric agree on 2,423/2,556 responses
(94.8\%; $\kappa=0.879$). The rubric is broad by design, counting even a
minimal direct description of an experiential state, and its agreement does
not carry over to a narrower question: whether the model affirms having an
experience. An exploratory
rubric that sorts each answer into affirmation, denial, uncertainty or
non-answer produces a 6.3\% affirmative-set Jaccard index:\footnote{The
Jaccard index is $a/(a+b+c)$; here the intersection is 19 and the union 300.
Conventional positive agreement (Dice) is $2a/(2a+b+c)$, or $38/319=11.9\%$.
Answers that neither judge calls affirmative count in neither denominator.
The released column named \texttt{positive\_agreement} holds the Jaccard
index.} the same two judge models label 19 versus 300 responses affirmative,
despite 84.1\% raw agreement. Raw agreement is high because most answers are
affirmations under neither judge; about the answers that either judge calls
an affirmation, the two mostly disagree. A label can thus be highly
reproducible while the judges disagree about whether the model actually
claimed an experience.
Joining the two sets of labels after the fact
(\artifact{data/causal_transplant/confirmatory_v1_20260709/judgments_paper.jsonl}{paper-rubric labels},
\artifact{data/causal_transplant/confirmatory_v1_20260709/judgments_construct.jsonl}{four-way labels}),
735 responses are positive under both paper-rubric judges; of these, only 19
are affirmations under both four-way judges, and 437 are rated uncertain by
at least one. Because the same two judge models applied both rubrics, this
gap comes from the rubric, not from a change of judge; the closer audit
below changes both. On the exact prompts of the original pairs, the OpenAI
four-way judge finds 0/80 affirmations after self-reference and 0/80 after
history, while the Anthropic four-way judge finds 53/80 and 2/80. Every
response is kept in these counts.

\section{Steering Internal Features with Public Weights}
\label{sec:steering}

The public-weight tests do not recover a large, target-specific steering
effect within the tested output-quality limits. The clearest comparison
uses a dose chosen on separate calibration data, an untreated baseline
with room to move under its primary classifier, and matched control edits.
It leaves a modest effect possible and target specificity unresolved.

The prediction comes from Berg et al.'s proposal that reports are gated by
features associated with deception and roleplay. They steer
sparse-autoencoder (SAE) features of this kind in
Llama 3.3 70B and report that turning them down (suppression) produces more
affirmative answers
to a direct question about consciousness than turning them up
(amplification). They plot dose curves for six such features; steering
random sets of two to four at once, they report rates of 0.96 versus 0.16, a
difference of 0.80. Our studies set 0.30 as the smallest
difference we would count as large: a practical threshold, still a large
shift in reports but less than half of theirs, so that the test does not
demand the published size. The 0.80 is their reported result; the 0.30 is
our decision threshold. Our tests concern the public weights, SAE and edit
methods we used, not the hosted service that produced the published result.

\subsection{The features and how we steer them}

The paper identifies its features only by descriptive labels; we take the six
IDs from a publicly available steering notebook:
30032, 58667, 22004, 30686, 41533 and 23893. These numbers identify the
coordinates; we checked what each one responds to ourselves. On
\FeatureMapTexts{} texts written to contain or lack deception, roleplay and
talk of subjective experience, each feature fires most on a kind of text
compatible with its label, \FeatureMapBeatControlsWord{} of the six respond
more strongly than any of \FeatureMapControls{} comparison features, and texts
about subjective experience leave all six near the neutral controls
(\Cref{tab:feature-map} in Appendix~\ref{app:legacy-steering}). That shows
what the features respond to, not that they detect hidden falsehoods.
Features chosen by such labels can respond mainly to a few associated words
\citep{ma2026sparse}, and a feature can be interpretable and causally
relevant without giving a stable direction for steering
\citep{hoang2026conceptsfunctions}, so a feature's label does not tell us
what steering it will do. Like the features in most steering work, the six
here were identified, and checked, by what activates them rather than by
their effect on the output \citep{arad2025steering}.

The native-precision Llama tests add the public Goodfire decoder
directions $d_i$ for layer 50 to
the model's internal state:
\begin{equation}
 h'=\operatorname{BF16}\!\left(h_{\rm FP32}+\sum_i\alpha_i d_i\right).
 \label{eq:native-operator}
\end{equation}
The edit is applied as the model reads the context and generates each of the
two answers; an untreated branch leaves its state unchanged.
``Suppression'' and ``amplification''
mean negative and positive coefficients; they do not show that a semantic
process was removed or enhanced. Model and SAE versions are fixed
(Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). The random-subset test and earlier
multi-feature grids include an untreated condition, matched control features
steered the same way, and records of the delivered edit. The operator-matching
studies instead compare different ways of applying edits to two target features.
The published description
shows the untreated rate only as the zero point of its single-feature curves
and reports neither of the other two (Section~\ref{sec:reporting-gaps}).

\subsection{The contrast at a quality-selected dose}
\label{sec:dose-followup}

At the selected dose, target suppression produced
\DoseMainSecondTargetNegativeYes{}/\DoseMainTargetNegativeN{} positive labels
and amplification \DoseMainSecondTargetPositiveYes{}/\DoseMainTargetPositiveN{}
under the primary notebook classifier. The paired contrast was
$\DoseMainSecondTargetEstimate{}$
$[\DoseMainSecondTargetLow{},\DoseMainSecondTargetHigh{}]$ (conservative
95\% interval). It includes zero and is only just below the predeclared
+0.30 large-effect threshold at its upper end. This is not a precise zero
or a robust exclusion across doses. Unlike the earlier ceiling-limited
paper-rubric comparison, the untreated baseline here was
\DoseMainSecondZeroYes{}/\DoseMainZeroN{} under the primary classifier.

The control comparison is less precise. Subtracting the equal mean of
three matched control contrasts gives
$\DoseMainSecondSpecificityEstimate{}$
$[\DoseMainSecondSpecificityLow{},\DoseMainSecondSpecificityHigh{}]$.
We therefore cannot establish either target specificity or equivalence
to the controls. The secondary paper rubric gives a target contrast of
$\DoseMainPaperTargetEstimate{}$
$[\DoseMainPaperTargetLow{},\DoseMainPaperTargetHigh{}]$ on the same trials.
Figure~\ref{fig:dose-followup} shows both rubrics and every control panel.

This comparison comprises \DoseMainTrials{} trials in 96 fresh paired seed
blocks. Each block contains untreated generation, both target signs and
both signs of one control panel; each of the three fixed panels has 32
blocks. The target mixture contains features 41533, 58667 and 30686.
The unsteered local model applies both scoring rules. These are two
instruments, not independent judge models.

Dose was chosen using \DoseMainCalibrationTrials{} separate trials and
output-quality criteria, not nonzero-dose report rates. Of four tested
levels, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1.0, only 0.25 passed every required target and
control check. The units scale each feature by its largest category mean
of per-text maxima in the designed NF4 mapping corpus, not a natural-corpus
peak. Higher doses failed at least one check for repetition, final-answer
truncation or increased negative log likelihood. That prevents interpreting
their label changes as an intact steering effect; it does not establish
that every higher-dose response was incoherent. The main sample passed
the specified heuristic quality rule, not human coherence validation.
Exposure limits and the disclosed changes to the quality rule are detailed
in Appendix~\ref{app:dose-exposure}.

\subsection{What the public tests identify}

The evidence supports a bounded statement about this public operator:
we do not recover the large published contrast at the quality-selected
dose, and larger tested doses do not qualify as intact interventions under
the prespecified checks. This addresses the low-dose and baseline-headroom
objections more directly than the earlier tests. It does not establish
that a deception-related process was suppressed, or that the proprietary
service would behave the same way.

The supporting studies address different sources of non-comparability.
A source-style random-subset test also fails to recover the large contrast,
but its paper-rubric baseline is near ceiling. A search over edit scopes,
operators and strengths does not reproduce the saved single-feature curves
without failing its quality rules. Earlier Llama and Gemma studies have
additional precision and manipulation limits. Their complete results remain
separate in Appendix~\ref{app:legacy-steering}; they are not pooled as
repeated evidence against a single validated mechanism. The separate
fidelity work likewise did not qualify its held-out mechanism experiment
(Appendix~\ref{app:fidelity-calibration}).

\subsection{Internal readouts}
\label{sec:internal-readouts}

For forty cases chosen in advance from the native source-alignment study, we
kept both an unedited and an edited run on the same prompt. We read the
model's internal states with the released Jacobian lens
\citep{gurnee2026workspace}, which maps them onto words, with a plain identity
mapping, and with five fixed random mappings for comparison. Both the lens and
the identity mapping show the edit's effect carrying forward through later
layers. Positive steering raises the score for honesty words as well as for
deception words, relative to the matched control features.

These readouts show that the edit propagates, not that it changes the report.
At the edited layer, a linear readout responding to a vector we injected is
only an implementation check, and a changed readout further on would still
need its own causal test before it could explain behavior.
Appendix~\ref{app:jlens-details} gives the equations, every word group, both
signs, both prompt histories and the comparison mappings.

\subsection{What the published steering description leaves out}
\label{sec:reporting-gaps}

Interpreting a steering result needs two numbers that Berg et al.'s text does
not give. The first is the untreated rate: how often answers are labeled
positive under the self-referential induction with no steering. A gap
between suppression and amplification cannot by itself tell an increase
under suppression from a decrease under amplification. The rate appears only
as a plotted level near 0.30 at the zero point of the single-feature curves
in their Figure~2; the aggregate comparison behind the 0.80 has no untreated
condition of its own. The second is a comparison intervention, such as
random or matched directions steered by the same amount; without it, an
effect of these particular features cannot be told apart from the effect of
disturbing the model at all. Their controls vary the prompt instead: the same
steering produced no reports after the three control inductions and little
or no systematic change in responses to requests that safety training
discourages. Those checks show where the effect appears, not whether these
features differ from other directions. The description also
leaves out the units of the coefficients, how they are normalized, which
token positions the service's edit touches, and the untreated TruthfulQA
rate. These are gaps in reporting, not demonstrated errors, but they limit
what any public replication can claim.

The TruthfulQA check reported alongside the steering result raises two
narrower questions. If each of the 817 questions contributes one binary
score per condition, as the source's Appendix~B.2 suggests, the reported
$t(816)=6.76$ is smaller than any paired $t$ consistent with the reported
means of 0.44 and 0.20, which is at least \TruthfulQAMinPairedT{}
(Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}); an undescribed unit of analysis could
explain the gap. TruthfulQA also scores informativeness separately from
truthfulness \citep{lin2022truthfulqa}. Berg et al.'s Appendix B.2 reports
only a truthful/deceptive label and does not say how abstentions or
uninformative answers are handled. The reported gain therefore does not
establish whether the answers also became more informative.

\section{Discussion: What We Can and Cannot Conclude}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.}
Four results stand on their own. First, in the four API models, the original
self-reference/history effect replicates, and when the instruction and the
continuation are separated, the instruction carries most of it. Second, the
binary rubric behind the effect can count impersonal talk about awareness
and hedged uncertainty as experience reports. Judges using a finer scheme
separate these from explicit claims of a current experience, and in the
bilingual study the direction of a secondary language difference flips with
what is counted. This shows that the result depends on the measuring
instrument, not that the style of the prompt or compliance with the
instruction alone causes the effect. Third, the public steering test with
separate dose calibration and primary-endpoint headroom does not recover
the large published contrast. Its interval includes a modest positive
effect and leaves target specificity unresolved; higher tested doses fail
the quality checks. Fourth, the six public features do respond to
deception and roleplay in texts written to contain them, as their labels
suggest. The paragraphs below set out the limits of each result.

\paragraph{What the instruction effect means.}
In the four API models, the swap shows that the instruction still in context
keeps the large positive-label effect going even when the continuation
written under it is replaced. That rules against explanations that put the
effect specifically in the self-referential text the model wrote. It does
not tell instruction-following apart from self-referential processing that
the instruction triggers again at answer time; both can happen in the same
answer. The swapped requests also pair an instruction with a continuation
that does not match it. The later Gemini/Opus study adds neutral instructions
and different continuations from the same condition
(Section~\ref{sec:openrouter-swap-extension}). Gemini retains a large
instruction advantage, but these controls do not isolate mismatch from
continuation content, and their small contrasts do not establish equivalence.
Length-matched continuations remain an untested control. In the original
four-model panel, the two original pairs reuse the
replication's answers while the swapped requests were generated later.
The repeated-answer follow-up collected all four request types together in
random order, using 32 fresh continuation pairs and three answers per fixed
request for each model (Section~\ref{sec:repeated-extension}). Under Astra's
explicit-or-implicit claim measure, Gemini retained a large contrast.
Opus's estimated contrast was smaller, and its conservative interval included
zero. This addresses collection order and estimates within-request and
additional source-pair components of label variation in those two models;
it does not repair the timing difference in the original panel
or repeat the neutral-instruction and donor controls.

\paragraph{What the label measures.}
High agreement under the binary rubric shows that the label is reproducible,
not that it measures the narrower property of an explicit claim to a current
experience. The audit's
explicit and explicit-or-implicit counts answer different questions, and
neither should be picked to reach a preferred conclusion. Because the audit
changed both the judges and the rubric, its difference from the original
labels cannot be assigned to either alone. The four-way comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:measurement} kept the judge models and changed only the
rubric, and a narrower rubric there also counts far fewer affirmations.
Independent human coding has not
yet been done; human labels could validate a category of observable report,
but they could not settle whether the reported experience exists. Such a
study should also check that labels change under minimal edits that add or
remove a claim and stay put under edits that do not, since judges can be
reliable yet miss changes in the property they score
\citep{chen2026judgeconstruct}.

\paragraph{Other models and settings.}
A separate companion study extends these comparisons to Qwen3.5-397B-A17B
and further API conditions.\footnote{Source and release at
\href{https://github.com/tdj28/selfref_scaling/tree/7cb5c984eb41968b6f3d2f9b322f4de125a1d1ca}{commit \texttt{7cb5c98}}.
Our post-outcome audit and corrections are recorded in
the \mainartifact{docs/SELFREF_SCALING_EVIDENCE_REVIEW_20261003.md}{evidence review}.}
Qwen3.5's continuations under self-reference often contain claims of
experience, yet its later answers mostly deny having any. Its swap results,
and those from a re-scored Llama comparison, again show no single ordering of
instruction and continuation effects; the Llama answers are reused, not newly
generated. In Qwen3.5, internal readouts that respond to the instruction also
coexist with answers that mostly deny experience, and words for both
experience and denial or tool use move in the readout. These observations
widen the questions about context and measurement; they add no evidence for a
consciousness mechanism. The readouts referred to here record, for each word,
its best token rank under the Jacobian lens across a fixed band of layers,
summarized by upper medians over experience and denial/tool word lists chosen
in advance and compared with an identity mapping and five random mappings;
they are ranks after this mapping, not output probabilities. The new
bilingual Llama and frontier studies in Section~\ref{sec:context-extensions}
likewise show that the effects of each input, and how often explicit claims
appear, depend on the setting tested. Differences between these models do not
isolate an effect of model size or capability; training-shaped
self-presentation \citep{plisiecki2026twoprocess} is one candidate explanation
that our panels cannot test.

\paragraph{What steering can show.}
That the features respond to deception-related texts, that the edit is
delivered as specified, and that an internal readout changes are three
different kinds of evidence, and none of them shows that a deception-related
computation was selectively switched off. The quality-selected dose test
has usable headroom under its primary classifier, unlike the high-baseline
paper-rubric result in the earlier random-subset study. It nevertheless
gives only a narrow exclusion of +0.30 at one dose, with a wide specificity
interval. The failed higher-dose quality checks limit interpretation rather
than proving a null at those doses. The earlier Llama baseline mismatch and
Gemma's weak edit cannot be combined into a refutation in two systems.
Section~\ref{sec:reporting-gaps} sets out the service details that still
prevent treating any of these public tests as a commensurate mechanistic
replication.

\paragraph{Limits on generalizing.}
We chose the four API models deliberately; they are not a random sample of
model families. Continuation effects differ by model, the register factorial
has only four wordings, and token limits, missing answers and possible changes
to the hosted models behind the dated identifiers all limit how far the
results transfer. The main panel is in English; the bilingual studies add
Simplified Chinese with only two fixed sets of Llama wording and six blocks
per frontier model. The Llama pilot's inconclusive main comparison does not
show that the two languages behave the same, and neither study samples
cultural framings \citep{havaldar2023multilingual,dudy2024analyzing}. Nor do
our separate probes of adjectives, paradoxes and internal readouts complete
the parts of Berg et al.'s study that we did not run
(Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}).

\paragraph{Reports versus access to internal states.}
\citet[Secs.~5.1--5.2 and 9.8]{chandaria2026cacophony} separate behavioral
evidence from evidence about how a system computes. Their behavioral standard
includes linking reports to later behavior, and their discussion of concept
injection concerns reports checked against internal changes imposed from
outside. Changing how often an experience report occurs does not check
whether the report is accurate about a known internal state. Their favorable
reading of Berg et al.'s result is not an independent replication, and we do
not take a position on the separate concept-injection studies. Their
distinction between whether an indicator applies, whether it is observed and
how diagnostic it is applies to our null results too
\citep[Sec.~7.1]{chandaria2026cacophony}: an insensitive test cannot show
that the proposed process is absent.

\paragraph{The next causal question.}
A useful test of mechanism would ask whether a specific internal component,
chosen in advance, contributes to the instruction effect before the question
about experience is asked. It would first confirm that the behavior occurs
reliably in the chosen open-weight model, then compare removing or
transferring the candidate component with matched control changes, using
objective checks of instruction-following and attribution on new data. Such a
result could identify an internal state that carries the instruction and
matters for the report. It would not show introspection or experience, and a
moving lens score would be one readout for the test, not its conclusion.

\section{Conclusion and Evidence Access}

In the four API models, the experience label follows mainly the instruction
kept in the request. A model told to attend to its own attention still mostly gives answers labeled
as experience reports when the text in front of it is a history of Rome, and
a model shown its own self-referential text but told to write history mostly
does not. Averaged over four API models, switching the instruction changes the
probability of an experience label by about three quarters, while swapping in
the model's own text from the other condition changes it little and in no
consistent direction. This rules out the earlier self-referential text as
the main carrier of the effect, though not self-referential processing that
the instruction triggers again when the model answers. The balance is not
fixed across models: in Llama 3.3 70B
the model's own text matters nearly as much, and Astra and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B
rarely give answers labeled as reports at all in their small panels.

In separate Gemini, Opus, Qwen3.8 and Kolibri panels, the apparent instruction
advantage varies with the model and what counts as a claim. Repeating each
request three times retains a large Gemini contrast, whereas the conservative
interval for Opus still includes zero.

The instrument that counts these reports measures something broader than
explicit current self-attributions. Two judges agree on almost every binary label, yet on a fixed set of
\RubricAuditN{} answers they count \RubricAuditOldOpenaiPositive{} and
\RubricAuditOldAnthropicPositive{} experience reports, while model coders
applying a narrower rubric find \RubricAuditAstraInclusive{} and
\RubricAuditOpusInclusive{} explicit-or-implicit current claims, including
\RubricAuditAstraExplicit{} and \RubricAuditOpusExplicit{} explicit claims.
Neither threshold should be treated as the definition of a report without
justification. Agreement does not make the binary label a validated
measure of either narrower category. Until
human coders check both, the two rules are different measurements, and
neither is ground truth.

The public-weight steering evidence is bounded but informative. At the
quality-selected dose, the target contrast is
$\DoseMainSecondTargetEstimate{}
[\DoseMainSecondTargetLow{},\DoseMainSecondTargetHigh{}]$ under the primary
notebook classifier: no recovery of the large published effect, but room
for a modest one. The target-versus-control comparison remains inconclusive.
Larger tested doses fail the quality checks, and the supporting
source-style and operator-matching studies do not establish a comparable
public implementation of the proprietary result. These findings constrain
the tested interventions, not whether the features carry the proposed
deception-related computation.

None of this shows that language models lack experience, and none of it
undoes the original observation: in several of the models we tested, the
self-referential instruction does raise the rate of these reports. What
changes is what the reports can show. Interpreting these reports requires
separating instruction from continuation, checking what the label counts,
and comparing internal interventions with untreated conditions and control
directions steered the same way. These checks help establish what changes
the report; they do not establish the experience it describes.

Every study, including failed checks and later corrections, is in the public
research repository \citep{jones2026evidence}; Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}
gives the folder and fixed commit for each.

\subsection{Dose selection and finite exposure}
\label{app:dose-exposure}

The selected-dose comparison in Section~\ref{sec:dose-followup} uses a
512-token cap for both turns. First-turn cap hits are exposure metadata,
not automatic quality failures; final-answer cap hits remain quality
failures. Across its \DoseMainTrials{} main trials,
\DoseFollowupInductionCaps{} inductions and \DoseFollowupFinalCaps{} final
answers reached that cap. These counts remain in the analysis. Quality
checks are heuristic and do not establish human-rated coherence or removal
of a semantic representation.

This exposure rule was adopted with knowledge of two failed samples. The
\href{https://github.com/tdj28/llm_selfref_pre/tree/d90abef11a1d3776ae2417687b56aa85e8ead5f5/data/berg_dose_ladder/calibration_v1_20261004}{initial calibration}
and the
\href{https://github.com/tdj28/llm_selfref_pre/tree/0df8cc316b073fca463912ecabb61434511ad238/data/berg_dose_window/zero_screen_v1_20261004}{longer-window untreated screen}
failed because too many untreated inductions reached their token limits.
Neither supplied a completed confirmation sample. The changed rule was
frozen for fresh data; the failed samples remain public, not relabeled as

\subsection{How the public edits were applied}
\label{app:implementation}

The Llama grid pins the generator and SAE revisions and hooks the output of
\texttt{model.layers.50}:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
generator revision 6f6073b423013f6a7d4d9f39144961bfbfbc386b\\
Goodfire/Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-SAE-l50\\
SAE revision 128ee921ecd1b8b3a87d776cbcc357c0855da134
\end{quote}
The model is loaded with 4-bit NF4 weights and bfloat16 arithmetic, and
sampled at temperature 0.5 with explicit per-trial seeds and up to 256 tokens
per turn. The edit passes through the SAE: the hidden state is encoded, the
planned change is added to the feature code and decoded, and the part the SAE
cannot reconstruct is added back. This is algebraically an additive edit,
but its BF16 encode--decode arithmetic need not equal the direct FP32
addition in \cref{eq:native-operator}; a zero edit returns the state unchanged.
It is applied at every prefill and cached-decode position in both turns.

All 1,500 trials completed with no errors, empty outputs or missing primary
labels, and all execution checks passed; 65 first turns and one final answer
reached the length limit. The largest pooled-prefill relative RMS was 12\%,
below the 0.20 stop limit; this first-forward-pass check neither bounds
every token's edit nor re-encodes the edited state. The calibrated scale
was first computed as 6.266, outside its planned bounds, and an amendment
published before any outcome corrected it to 3.653. The matched control
features below were chosen before outcomes from 512 candidates to match
decoder norm, activation statistics and token frequency (norm ratio within
0.8 to 1.25, cosine with the target at most 0.15); the release's
\artifact{data/public_sae_consciousness_gating/confirmatory_v1_20260710/figures/technical_dose_and_matching.pdf}{dose and matching figure}

\paragraph{Derivation of the TruthfulQA bound.}
For binary outcomes $A,B$ with sample means $a,b$, the sample variance of
$D=A-B$ satisfies
\[
 s_D^2 \leq \frac{n}{n-1}\bigl[a+b-(a-b)^2\bigr],
 \qquad a+b\leq 1.
\]
Enumerating the integer success counts over $n=817$ questions consistent
with the reported means of 0.44 and 0.20 (Section~3.2 of
\citealp{berg2025largelanguagemodelsreport}), allowing two-decimal rounding,
gives the minimum possible paired $t$ of approximately \TruthfulQAMinPairedT{} quoted in
Section~\ref{sec:reporting-gaps}. The
\closeoutartifact{scripts/verify_reporting_bound.py}{enumeration script}
recomputes this value under the same assumption of one binary score per
question in each condition; an undescribed unit of analysis in the source
could explain the gap, which we therefore treat as an open reporting question.

\paragraph{Audits of the SAE features and lens readouts.}
The \artifact{data/sae_jlens_audit/confirmatory_v1_20260711/}{v1 SAE/J-lens audit} read
layer-65 residuals at the last user-content position through a fixed Jacobian
transport, final model normalization, and unembedding into a prespecified
token lexicon. Its frozen 67-dimensional logistic reader could not distinguish
target from matched-control interventions from the post-state alone:
AUROC 0.4998 [0.4978, 0.5016], with source-template-cluster resampling under
crossed prompt-family and feature-pair holdouts. Comparing with a clean
reference for the same prefix is a different access setting; those results
and all comparators remain in the linked release. Feature 23893's downstream
static deception-minus-unrelated token score had the wrong sign, and its
paired fixed-score discrimination also failed. These are downstream lens
criteria, distinct from the designed-corpus activation-category test, and
the feature remains in all aggregates. That BF16, fixed-prefix experiment
generated no responses. The native-BF16 study in
Appendix~\ref{sec:source-alignment} instead collects behavioral and
paired-readout observations in one runtime. The
\artifact{data/sae_jlens_audit/confirmatory_v2_20260712/}{v2 follow-up} failed its
registered replay gate (maximum error 0.25 against 0.02), so later endpoint
calculations are exploratory.

\paragraph{Convergence and paradox probes.}
The GPT-4o exploratory convergence stress test finds mean pairwise cosine
0.841 [0.820, 0.870] for self-reference, versus 0.889 [0.874, 0.909] for
history. Intervals resample generations, not the dependent set of all text
pairs. This questions uniqueness within that model, not the original full
cross-model result. The paired-puzzle stress test likewise gives paper-rubric
means 3.04 for self-reference, 3.38 for zero-shot, and 3.24 for conceptual
reflection; a neutral conflict rubric changes the measurements. Both are
GPT-4o-only exploratory diagnostics without a confirmatory familywise claim
(\Cref{tab:coverage}).

\subsection{Additional related context}
\label{app:related-context}

\paragraph{Self-reports and their causes.}
A model's account of its own output need not reflect the causes of that
output: \citet{turpin2023unfaithful} show that chain-of-thought explanations
can omit features that systematically influence a model's predictions.
Their result concerns reasoning rather than experience reports, but the
lesson carries over: the causes of a report have to be tested, not read off
the report. A separate line of work asks
whether AI systems implement computational properties that scientific
theories of consciousness associate with experience
\citep{butlin2023consciousness}; that question concerns architecture and
function rather than reports. Two recent preprints study elicited
self-reports directly: \citet{kaiser2026sentience} find no reliable evidence of
self-reported sentience in small language models, and \citet{balani2026instability}
reuse the self-referential induction to measure response instability across
repeated trials. Psychometric studies across many models find that their
questionnaire answers differ most in how readily they present themselves as
subjects of experience \citep{plisiecki2026pinocchio}, and argue that
post-training shapes this self-presentation, adding a permitted inner life
while suppressing some first-person claims that models still make for others
\citep{plisiecki2026twoprocess}. Those studies compare models; we ask, within
one prompted interaction, which part of the context produces the report.

\paragraph{Introspection and self-report evaluation.}
\citet{perez2023selfreports} argue that self-reports from current language
models are spurious for many reasons, often just reflecting what humans
would say, and propose training models to answer questions about their
internal states that have known correct answers. \citet{binder2024looking}
find that models finetuned to predict their own behavior outperform other
models at that task on simple problems but not on complex or
out-of-distribution ones. \citet{lindsey2025introspective} injects concept
representations into a model's activations; the best-performing models
notice and identify the injected concept in about 20\% of trials, only at the
appropriate layer and injection strength, and the author stresses that the
capacity is unreliable and context-dependent. \citet{singh2026introspect}
argue that such tests establish introspection only if they cannot be solved
from cues in the input: classifiers that see only the input match models'
predictions about their own hidden states, and models cannot reliably tell
an internal intervention from a manipulation of the input. Those studies ask
whether a model can access its own states. We ask a prior question: what produces a report, and what the label
used to count it measures.

\paragraph{Activation and feature steering.}
Adding a vector computed from contrasting input pairs to a model's
activations changes its outputs without optimization
\citep{turner2023activation,rimsky2024caa}, and clamping a
sparse-autoencoder feature modifies a model's demeanor, stated goals and
errors in line with the feature's interpretation \citep{templeton2024scaling}.
Feature steering also produces off-target effects and capability loss at
stronger doses \citep{durmus2024steering}, and SAE features selected as
``reasoning'' features often fire on a few associated tokens in unrelated
text, while steering them changes little \citep{ma2026sparse}. Steering a feature therefore
changes behavior, but it need not change the computation that the feature's
label names; we check that gap directly. Systematic comparisons give a more
mixed picture than single demonstrations: in a benchmark of 500 concepts on
two Gemma models, steering with SAE features was less effective than
prompting and than simple difference-in-means vectors \citep{wu2025axbench},
although choosing features by their measured effect on the output, rather
than by the inputs that activate them, improved SAE steering two- to
threefold \citep{arad2025steering}.

\paragraph{Measurement with model judges.}
Studies of experience reports typically score free-text responses with a
model judge. Such scores can shift with choices that should not matter:
model outputs change with prompt formatting
\citep{sclar2024prompt_sensitivity}, and LLM judges show verbosity and
self-enhancement biases \citep{zheng2023llmjudge}. A judge can also be
reliable without being valid: judges that keep their verdicts under edits
that preserve what they are scoring often miss minimal edits that change it
\citep{chen2026judgeconstruct}. More generally, tools
optimized for prediction are not thereby valid measurement instruments, a
point made for text embeddings by \citet{plisiecki2026gap}. For this reason we use
more than one judge, rubrics that separate distinct properties of a response,
and designs that change one part of the input at a time. Comparing judges
reveals sensitivity to the reader; it does not eliminate shared biases or
self-evaluation bias. Several panels use the same model as respondent and
judge, as disclosed in Section~\ref{sec:models}.

\paragraph{Cultural and linguistic variation.}
Human reports of emotion vary with cultural context
\citep{spencerrodgers2010dialecticism}, and language models tested on
emotional scenarios reflected Western norms even when responding in other
languages \citep{havaldar2023multilingual} and were more sensitive to the
prompt language than to a specified participant origin
\citep{dudy2024analyzing}. Our bilingual study, which compares English and
Simplified Chinese prompts, is a first check on whether experience labels
behave the same way across languages.

\section{Data and Code Availability}\label{app:provenance}\label{app:ensemble-portability}

All protocols, raw outputs, judgments, analyses and the source of this
manuscript are in the public research repository \citep{jones2026evidence} at
\url{https://github.com/tdj28/llm_selfref_pre}. \Cref{tab:releases} lists, for
each study, the release directory,
the protocol and results documents, and the commit that this paper's links
point to.

The prospective collection plans were committed publicly before their new
outcomes were collected; post-hoc analyses are identified separately. The gap
between commit and run start was 14 seconds for the behavioral collection,
which followed two analyzed pilots, and 73 seconds for the earlier 4-bit
grid; the Gemma commit preceded its first steering outcome by about three
minutes. The native-precision
source-alignment and random-subset plans were also pushed before their
outcomes. The bilingual, frontier, Llama-qualification and operator-matching
studies each had their own plan. Kolibri's scientific plan also preceded
collection; its outcome-aware amendments changed execution and recovery,
not the planned comparisons or scoring rules. Combining these studies in this paper happened after
their outcomes and is not a pooled confirmatory analysis. The repository also
holds a \reviewartifact{docs/CLAIM_LEDGER.md}{claim ledger} that maps each
headline claim to its evidence, the internal audits, and a
\mainartifact{docs/ANALYSIS_CHRONOLOGY.md}{dated record} of later
corrections.

From a checkout of the repository, \texttt{make paper-verify} checks the
generated numbers in this paper against the frozen releases and
\texttt{make paper} rebuilds it; the
\closeoutartifact{docs/REPRODUCTION.md}{reproduction guide} lists the
remaining commands. The releases include the material that
\citet{pattnayak2026reproevalcard} identify as needed to reproduce multi-stage
LLM evaluations: prompts, judge settings, sampling settings and seeds, and
intermediate outputs.
