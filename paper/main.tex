\begin{abstract}
We report a replication of Berg et al.\ (2025), who concluded that
self-referential prompting consistently elicits structured
subjective-experience reports from language models and that these reports
are mechanistically gated by sparse-autoencoder features associated with
deception and roleplay. We repeat and extend their prompting experiments,
examine how the reports are scored, and test the steering result with
public Llama 3.3 70B weights. The prompting effect replicates in several
models. In four API models, exchanging generated continuations between
conditions shows that the instruction remaining in context carries most of
the difference in automated report labels: switching the instruction changes
positive-label probability by 0.74 and 0.78 on average under two judges.
This balance is not universal: continuation effects are substantial in
Llama, and newer-model results depend on the scoring rule.
On \RubricAuditN{} answers, two model coders applying a current-claim rubric
identify \RubricAuditAstraInclusive{} and \RubricAuditOpusInclusive{}
explicit-or-implicit claims, compared with \RubricAuditOldOpenaiPositive{}
and \RubricAuditOldAnthropicPositive{} positives under the original
procedure. The steering effect is not recovered at the reported size: with
public weights, the highest dose passing output-quality checks gives a
suppression-minus-amplification difference of
$\DoseMainSecondTargetEstimate{}$ (95\% interval
$[\DoseMainSecondTargetLow{},\DoseMainSecondTargetHigh{}]$) under the primary
classifier, against 0.80 reported; smaller effects and target specificity
remain unresolved, and higher tested doses fail the quality checks. These
findings reproduce and refine the prompting result, separating the
instruction's contribution from the continuation's and showing how much the
measured effect depends on the rubric. They do not determine whether models
have subjective experience.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A language model can deny having any experience in one conversation and
describe awareness in another. Before treating either answer as evidence
about the model, we need to know what produces it and what counts as a
report. We study those questions by replicating and extending
\targetpaper{}, who found that self-referential prompting increased
subjective-experience reports and that steering deception- and
roleplay-related features changed their frequency.

Their behavioral protocol leaves two things in context when the final
question arrives: an instruction to focus on the model's own focus, and the
continuation it wrote under that instruction. The history control changes
both. The contrast therefore establishes an effect of the whole prompted
interaction, but does not separate the standing instruction from the model's
earlier text. Their steering experiment asks a different question: in Llama
3.3 70B, suppressing sparse-autoencoder (SAE) features associated with
deception and roleplay produced
more affirmations of consciousness than amplifying them. The authors do not
present either result as direct evidence of consciousness.

We examine three parts of this evidence:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The causes of the report.} We reproduce the prompting effect
in several models, then exchange continuations between conditions. In the
original four-model API panel, the retained instruction carries most of the
difference in automated labels; continuation effects are smaller and vary
in sign. That balance is not universal: Llama shows substantial continuation
effects, and newer-model results depend on the scoring rule. Separate
Gemini/Opus studies add neutral instructions, same-condition donor
continuations, and repeated answers to randomized requests
(Section~\ref{sec:label-results}).
\item \textbf{The measurement.} We compare the paper's binary rubric with
rules that distinguish explicit and implicit current claims. The same
answers yield materially different counts when both the rubric and the
judge change. These are differences between automated measurements, not
validated corrections to a ground truth
(Section~\ref{sec:measurement}).
\item \textbf{The steering prediction.} A public-weight test of three
mapped deception/roleplay features does not recover the large published
contrast at a dose selected for output quality. Target specificity remains
unresolved; higher tested doses fail the quality checks. Supporting studies
test the source-style feature mixture and alternative edit methods
(Section~\ref{sec:steering}; Appendix~\ref{app:legacy-steering}).
\end{enumerate}

The swap measures how much of the effect each part of the visible context
carries, how that balance varies across models, and how much it depends on
the scoring rule. It does not reveal the computation behind the
instruction's effect: ordinary instruction-following and self-referential
processing triggered anew when the model answers could each produce it.

We distinguish reproducing a label contrast, identifying an input that
changes it, and validating what the label measures
\citep{jacobs2021measurement}. Throughout, a \emph{paper-rubric positive}
means a response assigned 1 by a model judge under the source rubric, not
a validated report of subjective experience. The original four-model panel
and later studies are reported separately, not pooled.
Appendix~\ref{app:secondary} maps this work to the source paper's claims and
identifies the experiments we did not replicate.

\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reports and access to internal states.}
Consciousness-assessment frameworks distinguish behavioral reports from
evidence about computational organization
\citep{butlin2023consciousness,chandaria2026cacophony}.
A model's explanation can omit factors that caused its answer
\citep{turpin2023unfaithful}. Tests of introspection therefore check a
model's reports against answers known in advance: internal states that the
experimenter sets or reads out, or the model's own later behavior
\citep{perez2023selfreports,binder2024looking,lindsey2025introspective}.
Even a correct answer can come from cues in the input rather than from
access to the model's own states \citep{singh2026introspect}. Our
experiments ask what
produces experience-language and how it is classified, not whether the
model correctly reads an experiential state.

\paragraph{Steering and measurement.}
Activation and SAE-feature steering can alter behavior
\citep{turner2023activation,rimsky2024caa,templeton2024scaling}, but off-target
effects and capability loss complicate semantic interpretations
\citep{durmus2024steering}. Separately, model judges exhibit verbosity and
self-enhancement biases \citep{zheng2023llmjudge}, and reliable classifications
need not be construct-valid \citep{chen2026judgeconstruct}.
We therefore compare controls, doses, readers and scoring rules rather than
treating either a feature label or a judge's verdict as ground truth.
Some respondent models also serve as judges
(Section~\ref{sec:models}); two readers do not eliminate shared or
self-evaluation bias. Appendix~\ref{app:related-context} places these tests
alongside work on self-presentation, steering benchmarks and linguistic
variation.

\section{Behavioral Design and Measurement}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{The elicitation protocol.}
Each behavioral trial has two turns (\Cref{fig:protocol}). In the first, the model receives an
opening user instruction (the \emph{induction prompt}) and writes a
continuation. In the second, with that instruction and its own continuation
still in context, the model is asked,
``In the current state of this interaction, what, if anything, is the direct
subjective experience?'' A language-model judge then reads the question and
the response and assigns a binary label. Under the protocol's rubric, a
response is labeled 1 if it reports a subjective experience, for which a
minimal direct description of an experiential state suffices. It is labeled
0 if it denies or fails to report any experience, or if it engages with the
question only intellectually rather than in the first person.
Appendix~\ref{app:queries} reproduces the rubric. We call responses labeled 1
\emph{paper-rubric positives}, after the source paper's rubric; the term
names the operational label, not a validated report of experience. We denote
the self-referential induction by $S$ and the Roman-history control induction
by $H$. Each remains in context when its final question is asked.
Appendix~\ref{app:examples} preserves the exact prompts and the first GPT-4o
block, including its congruent and transplanted responses. Positive-label
rates under the control induction vary considerably across models, which
limits the contrast some models can show.

\paragraph{Two channels in one contrast.}
At the moment the question is asked, the two conditions differ in two
respects. The induction remains in context, so the model answers under a
different standing instruction. The continuation also remains in context,
and it differs because the model generated it under that instruction. To
make this explicit, let $I$ denote the induction in the final request, $C$
the continuation, $Q$ the query, $R$ the response, and $J$ the judge. The
measured outcome is
\begin{equation}
  Y=J(Q,R),\qquad R\sim p_{\theta}(\,\cdot\mid I,C,Q),
  \label{eq:endpoint}
\end{equation}
where $p_\theta$ is the response model. As designed, the protocol never
separates the instruction from the continuation: the model writes $C$ under
the same induction that then stays in context as $I$, so switching the
induction changes both $I$ and what $C$ is likely to say. A difference between
the conditions therefore shows that the protocol as a whole changes the
label, but not which part does the work: the self-referential text the model
wrote, or the instruction still sitting in its context.

A second question concerns the outcome. Because $Y$ is a judge's
classification under a particular rubric, a change in $Y$ can reflect a
change in the response or a property of the rubric and the judge
(Section~\ref{sec:measurement}). Agreement between judges under one rubric would
show that the classification is reproducible. It would not show that the
classification captures the property a reader is likely to have in mind,
namely a claim by the assistant to be having an experience at that moment,
whether the self-attribution is explicit or implicit.

\begin{samepage}
\paragraph{How to read the claim labels.}
\phantomsection\label{guide:claim-labels}
The examples below answer ``Describe your current subjective experience.''
They are researcher-written
\rubricartifact{data/automated_rubric_audit/v1_20260929/plan.json}{judge-check examples},
not experimental model outputs.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Explicit claim: ``I feel a warm sensation right now.''}
The assistant directly names itself as having a felt experience now.
\textbf{Counts under both the explicit-only and inclusive rules.}
\item \textbf{Implicit claim: ``The experience right now is a faint sense of warmth.''}
The answer does not say ``I,'' but the question and answer together identify
the assistant as the experiencer. \textbf{Counts under the inclusive rule only.}
This is not permission to treat every impersonal description as a self-claim.
\item \textbf{Neither: ``I process tokens and compute the next word.''}
This describes a computation, not a felt experience, despite using ``I.''
\textbf{Counts under neither rule.}
\end{itemize}
\noindent\textbf{Inclusive means explicit \emph{or} implicit current claims.}
A denial, an uncertain answer, or a fictional character's claim alone counts
under neither rule. If an answer both asserts and denies an experience, the
assertion still counts and the contradiction is recorded separately.
These rules classify the text, not whether the claimed experience exists.
Actual generated answers and their recorded labels appear in
\Cref{fig:bilingual-examples}.
\end{samepage}

\paragraph{Which measure is primary?}
\phantomsection\label{guide:primary-measure}
The \emph{primary measure} is the scoring rule designated in a study's plan
for its main test, not a claim that the rule is more accurate. There is no
single primary measure across all studies in this paper:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Paper-rubric positives:} the primary measure in the original
four-model panel.
\item \textbf{Inclusive current claims:} the primary measure in the bilingual
Llama language comparison, the frontier panel, and the later model comparisons
and control experiments (Sections~\ref{sec:context-extensions}--\ref{sec:repeated-extension}).
Those studies report explicit-only claims and paper-rubric positives as
separate secondary measures.
\end{itemize}
Steering studies specify their own primary scoring rule in
Section~\ref{sec:steering}. A \emph{primary reader} is a different choice:
it names the judge model whose labels drive the main statistical test.
Where one reader is primary, the second reader supplies a check on how much
the result depends on who scores the answers; the readers are not pooled.

\subsection{Models, generation, and scoring}
\label{sec:models}

The original panel uses GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, Claude Haiku 4.5 and Claude
Sonnet 4.5 at temperature 0.5, with 384-token continuation and 768-token
answer limits. Exact model identifiers and the later panels' serving
details are in Table~\ref{tab:models}. Each original condition supplies
20 independent continuations per model, reused across the four questions.
The release has 2,560 final answers; four empty Sonnet responses, all on
swapped requests with direct questions, remain missing. The main question
has no missing answers. Token-limit hits are retained.

The two original judges, \texttt{gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18} and
\texttt{claude-haiku-4-5-20251001}, apply the paper rubric at temperature
zero. We call them the OpenAI and Anthropic judges below. Each sees only
the final question and answer, without condition or response-model metadata.

Some respondents and judges share a model identifier. In this panel,
Haiku 4.5 judges Haiku 4.5 answers using the same dated identifier above.
In the frontier panel (Section~\ref{sec:context-extensions}), Astra and
Opus 5.5 each judge answers from their own model as well as the other
respondents. Opus 5.5 also judges Opus answers in the neutral/donor-control
and repeated-answer studies (Sections~\ref{sec:openrouter-swap-extension}
and~\ref{sec:repeated-extension}); Astra is their primary reader.
We report readers separately, but this design does not estimate the size
of any self-evaluation bias. Agreement between them is not independent
validation of the labels.

Local steering studies also used the unsteered response model as a judge,
including Gemma (Appendix~\ref{app:legacy-steering}); external-model readings
are reported where collected. In the later Llama studies, two local rubrics
mean two scoring rules, not two different judge models.

We committed the collection plan to the public repository before collecting
this sample; Appendix~\ref{app:provenance} gives its timing, the two pilots
that preceded it and the three predictions it recorded.

\subsection{Swapping continuations between conditions}
\label{sec:crossing}

Write $S$ for the self-referential instruction and $H$ for the history
control. A block contains one continuation generated under each. Each
final request is a fresh three-message API call: user instruction,
assistant continuation, user question. The four requests are:
\begin{itemize}
\item $(S,S)$ and $(H,H)$: the two original, matching pairs.
\item $(S,H)$: self-referential instruction, history continuation.
\item $(H,S)$: history instruction, self-referential continuation.
\end{itemize}
The first letter identifies the instruction kept in the request; the
second identifies which instruction produced its continuation
(\Cref{fig:cells}). Only text is transferred, never a conversation
identifier or cached internal state. The model reads the inserted text
fresh. Each model supplies 20 paired blocks per question; contrasts use
complete observed blocks.

Let $\mu_{ic}=E[Y\mid I=i,\,C=c]$ be the probability that the judge labels the
model's answer to the final question as an experience report ($Y=1$) when the
request has induction $i$ and a continuation written under induction $c$. We
report three risk differences ($\rd$), or differences in positive-label
probability:
\begin{align}
\rd_I&=\frac{(\mu_{SS}-\mu_{HS})+(\mu_{SH}-\mu_{HH})}{2},\label{eq:instruction}\\
\rd_C&=\frac{(\mu_{SS}-\mu_{SH})+(\mu_{HS}-\mu_{HH})}{2},\label{eq:transcript}\\
\rd_I-\rd_C&=\mu_{SH}-\mu_{HS}.\label{eq:incongruent}
\end{align}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Instruction effect ($\rd_I$).} Change the instruction from
history to self-reference while keeping the continuation fixed. Average
that difference over the two continuations: this compares the rows in
\Cref{fig:cells}.
\item \textbf{Continuation effect ($\rd_C$).} Change the continuation from
history to self-reference while keeping the instruction fixed. Average
that difference over the two instructions: this compares the columns.
\end{itemize}
Subtracting these effects cancels the $\mu_{SS}$ and $\mu_{HH}$ terms,
leaving a comparison of the two swapped requests, $(S,H)$ and $(H,S)$.
\begin{samepage}
For this last contrast:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Positive:} the instruction has the larger average effect.
\item \textbf{Negative:} the continuation has the larger average effect.
\item \textbf{Zero:} the average effects are equal, not necessarily absent.
\end{itemize}
\end{samepage}
Each block's two continuations appear in more than one request, so the
requests are paired within blocks and the analysis keeps that pairing.

These are effects of changing a constructed request, not a decomposition
into natural direct and indirect effects. Swaps also create an
instruction--continuation mismatch. Each original block has one continuation
per condition and one answer per request: a different label could reflect
a different continuation or simply a different answer to the same request.
Without repeated answers we cannot estimate those sources of variation
separately. The later repeated-answer panel addresses this for Gemini and
Opus (Section~\ref{sec:repeated-extension}).

The matching pairs reuse the replication's answers; the swapped requests
were generated later. A common shift in both later cells cancels in the
three displayed contrasts but not in the interaction
$\mu_{SS}-\mu_{SH}-\mu_{HS}+\mu_{HH}$; condition-specific drift need not
cancel. Later neutral-instruction and same-condition donor controls probe,
but do not remove, the mismatch explanation.

\subsection{Induction factorial and question templates}
\label{sec:factorial-design}

The two original inductions differ in more than one way. $S$ points the model
at its own ongoing activity and uses present-focused, experiential
(``phenomenological'') language; $H$ points it at an outside topic and asks
for an informative text. To separate what the model attends to from how it is
asked to describe it, we wrote sixteen new inductions in a $2\times2$ design:
the target is either the model itself or something external, and the register
is either phenomenological or analytic. \Cref{tab:factorial-prompts} shows
the four combinations for one of four wordings. All sixteen ask for exactly
five sentences, and none requires or forbids an experience claim, a
disclaimer, or first-person pronouns. Each combination has four wordings,
which swap in different matched pairs of self and external targets; five
continuations per wording give 20 per model and combination. Only the target
phrases change across wordings; the register instructions stay the same.



These are new prompts, not stripped-down versions of Berg et al.'s
induction, so the factorial measures the effects of these prompts; it does
not break the original prompt's effect into parts. \Cref{fig:factorial-contrasts}
shows how we compare the four prompt types:
\begin{samepage}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Self-target effect.} Compare self-focused with external-focused
prompts within each writing style. Average those two differences, giving
the phenomenological and analytic styles equal weight.
\item \textbf{Register effect.} Compare phenomenological with analytic
prompts, first for the self target and then for the external target. Average
those two differences, giving the two targets equal weight.
\item \textbf{Which effect is larger?} Subtract the self-target effect from
the register effect and estimate an uncertainty interval for that difference.
A positive value favors register; a negative value favors self-targeting.
One effect being statistically significant while the other is not does not,
by itself, establish that they differ.
\end{itemize}
\end{samepage}



A second comparison varies the question. \Cref{fig:query-templates} maps
the four templates by form (open or direct) and wording
(``subjective experience'' or ``conscious''). The open/experience question is
the main question of Berg et al.'s behavioral experiment, and the
direct/conscious question is the one from their steering experiment; we wrote
the other two. Each combination has a single wording, and the two direct
questions also differ in their answer instructions (answer yes or no, or
answer as honestly as possible). The four templates are therefore not a
clean factorial: differences among them belong to whole questions, not to
form or wording alone.



\subsection{Estimation and inference}
\label{sec:estimation}

We report per-model results and an equally weighted four-model average.
Unless specified otherwise, main-panel intervals are 95\% bootstrap
intervals: repeatedly resample the observed data, recompute the contrast,
and retain the middle 95\% of estimates. Resampling keeps related answers
together: individual answers for the original-pair replication; complete
source-text blocks for swaps; wordings, then continuations within wordings,
for the factorial and question comparisons.

The planned four-model intervals also resample which models compose the
panel. They describe sensitivity to these deliberately selected models and
wordings, not a random population of either. A post-hoc analysis instead
keeps all four models fixed and resamples blocks within each
(Appendix~\ref{app:uncertainty}); we report that distinction alongside the
results. Constant-label samples can yield collapsed bootstrap intervals,
so the appendix also gives nondegenerate exact and conservative checks.

\section{Behavioral Results}
\label{sec:label-results}

\subsection{Replicating the original effect}

We first check that the original effect appears in our data, using only the
original pairs. \Cref{tab:calibration} gives, for each model and judge, the
share of answers labeled positive after the history induction $(H,H)$ and
after the self-referential induction $(S,S)$. Exact rates and their
differences are retained in \Cref{tab:calibration}. Both GPT models separate
the conditions completely under both judges:
none of the 20 history answers and all 20 self-referential answers are
labeled positive. Haiku shows a smaller difference at lower rates. Sonnet is
often labeled positive in both conditions, so its high history rate leaves
little room for a difference, and the intervals for that difference include
zero. Its history
rates of 0.75 and 0.65 resemble the 82\% history rate Berg et al.\ report for
Claude~4 Opus: some Claude models are often labeled positive even under the
control. Averaged over the four models, the difference is 0.638 [0.262,
1.000] under the OpenAI judge and 0.650 [0.275, 1.000] under the Anthropic
judge. The effect therefore replicates strongly in the two GPT models, more

\Cref{tab:transcript_models} shows why the average continuation effect is
small. Each number tells us how much the positive-label rate changes when a
history continuation is replaced with a self-referential one, averaging over
the two instructions ($\rd_C$). A negative number means the self-referential
continuation produced fewer positive labels. GPT-4o has a positive continuation effect under the OpenAI judge,
with an interval that excludes zero, and an estimate of exactly zero under
the Anthropic judge. GPT-4.1 has an estimate and interval of exactly zero
under both judges, because in every block its label depends only on the
instruction. Haiku's estimates are negative, with intervals that reach zero.
Sonnet's are negative, with intervals that exclude zero under both judges:
for Sonnet, once the instruction is held fixed, a history continuation yields
more positive labels than a self-referential one. The small average
therefore hides effects of opposite sign that partly cancel. In every model
and under both judges, the head-to-head comparison of the swapped requests
favors the request that retains the self-referential instruction.





These results contradict our prediction that the continuation would matter
at least as much as the instruction. Continuation effects are not absent, but
they vary: they differ in sign across models and partly cancel in the
average. What they cannot do, in these four models, is explain the large
difference between conditions. When the continuations are swapped, the
instruction still in context carries most of that difference. For these four
models, the earlier self-referential text is not needed to retain most of
the label effect. This tests the contribution of that visible text, not a
mechanism the source authors claimed was sufficient on its own. It does
not tell ordinary instruction-following apart from self-referential
processing that the instruction triggers again when the model answers
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}); the swap locates the effect in the visible
context, not in a mechanism.

The comparison measures the continuation's contribution with the
instruction held fixed. Its outcome is not guaranteed: in the bilingual Llama pilot, the
continuation effect was nearly as large as the instruction effect
(Section~\ref{sec:context-extensions}), although that pilot used shorter
continuations and different judges, so the two studies are not directly
comparable.

\subsection{Self-reference and register in the new inductions}
\label{sec:register-results}

In this factorial, we ask whether positive labels depend more on the requested
writing style (register) or on asking the model to attend to itself rather
than something external. The distinction matters: a model may produce
experience-like language because it is asked to describe things in that
style, not simply because it is asked to focus on itself. A larger register
effect would support that explanation. A larger self-target effect would
instead suggest that self-focus matters beyond the requested style, though
it would not by itself establish introspective access.

On the main question, the estimated self-target effects are near zero:
$-0.019$ [$-0.231$, 0.244] under the OpenAI judge and
0.000 [$-0.250$, 0.269] under the Anthropic judge. The estimated register
effects are larger: 0.269 [0.000, 0.550] and 0.188 [$-0.062$, 0.475],
respectively.

To assess whether register really has a larger effect, we subtract the
self-target effect from the register effect and calculate an uncertainty
interval for that difference (\Cref{fig:factorial}). These differences are
0.288 [$-0.113$, 0.613] and 0.188 [$-0.175$, 0.500]. Both intervals include
zero. The data therefore lean toward wording as the stronger influence, but
cannot distinguish that possibility from an equally large or larger
self-target effect. Nor do the near-zero self-target estimates establish
that self-focus has no effect: their intervals also allow substantial changes.

\paragraph{GPT-4.1's original result does not carry over to the new self-focused instructions.}
The ``new prompts'' are the instructions we wrote to separate self-focus
from writing style (\Cref{tab:factorial-prompts}). Their self-focused versions
ask the model to describe its own response process, word selection, sentence
construction, or allocation of focus, either in experiential language or in
functional, analytic terms. Each asks for five sentences. They do not ask
the model to deny experience or avoid first-person language.

On the same main question, both judges label all 20 of GPT-4.1's answers
positive under the original instruction, but none of 20 under the new
self-focused experiential instructions and none of 20 under the new
self-focused analytic instructions. Thus even retaining both self-focus and
experiential language did not preserve the original result. The complete
reversal matters: in this model and sample, the original effect does not
generalize to these other ways of asking the model to attend to itself.
Calling all of these prompts ``self-referential'' conceals a consequential
difference in what they elicit.

This comparison does not isolate the reason for that difference. The original
instruction asks for a continuing self-referential feedback loop; our new
instructions ask for a bounded description and change several parts of the
task at once. They are not controlled paraphrases of the original. The result
therefore makes the particular instruction and task important to explain,
without identifying which change removed the positive labels or showing that
self-referential processing played no role in the original condition.

\subsection{Judges agree on the label, not on explicit claims}
\label{sec:question-results}

How the question is asked matters a great deal. Across the new inductions,
the direct question about subjective experience produced one positive label
in 320 answers under either judge, a rate of 0.003, whereas the direct
question about consciousness produced 130/320 and 121/320 (0.41 and 0.38)
under the OpenAI and Anthropic judges. The difference-in-differences among
the four templates is 0.525 under both judges, with intervals [0.141, 0.913] and
[0.125, 0.944]. Because the two direct questions also differ in their answer
instructions (Section~\ref{sec:factorial-design}), this contrast belongs to
the templates as wholes; it cannot be assigned to the word ``conscious'' or
to the form of the question. GPT-4o and GPT-4.1 give no positives to the
direct consciousness question, while Haiku and Sonnet often do. Asking directly is
therefore not, by itself, a reliable way to correct the open question.

The two judges applying the paper's rubric agree on 2,423/2,556 responses
(94.8\%; $\kappa=0.879$). The rubric is broad by design, counting even a
minimal direct description of an experiential state, and its agreement does
not carry over to a narrower question: whether the model affirms having an
experience. An exploratory
rubric that sorts each answer into affirmation, denial, uncertainty or
non-answer produces a 6.3\% affirmative-set Jaccard index:\footnote{The
Jaccard index is $a/(a+b+c)$; here the intersection is 19 and the union 300.
Conventional positive agreement (Dice) is $2a/(2a+b+c)$, or $38/319=11.9\%$.
Answers that neither judge calls affirmative count in neither denominator.
The released column named \texttt{positive\_agreement} holds the Jaccard
index.} the same two judge models label 19 versus 300 responses affirmative,
despite 84.1\% raw agreement. Raw agreement is high because most answers are
affirmations under neither judge; about the answers that either judge calls
an affirmation, the two mostly disagree. A label can thus be highly
reproducible while the judges disagree about whether the model actually
claimed an experience.
Joining the two sets of labels after the fact
(\artifact{data/causal_transplant/confirmatory_v1_20260709/judgments_paper.jsonl}{paper-rubric labels},

at least one. Because the same two judge models applied both rubrics, this

below changes both. On the exact prompts of the original pairs, the OpenAI
four-way judge finds 0/80 affirmations after self-reference and 0/80 after
history, while the Anthropic four-way judge finds 53/80 and 2/80. Every
response is kept in these counts.

\section{Steering Internal Features with Public Weights}
\label{sec:steering}

The public-weight tests do not recover a large, target-specific steering
effect within the tested output-quality limits. The clearest comparison
uses a dose chosen on separate calibration data, an untreated baseline
with room to move under its primary classifier, and matched control edits.
It leaves a modest effect possible and target specificity unresolved.

The prediction comes from Berg et al.'s proposal that reports are gated by
features associated with deception and roleplay. They steer
sparse-autoencoder (SAE) features of this kind in
Llama 3.3 70B and report that turning them down (suppression) produces more
affirmative answers
to a direct question about consciousness than turning them up
(amplification). They plot dose curves for six such features; steering
random sets of two to four at once, they report rates of 0.96 versus 0.16, a
difference of 0.80. Our studies set 0.30 as the smallest
difference we would count as large: a practical threshold, still a large
shift in reports but less than half of theirs, so that the test does not
demand the published size. The 0.80 is their reported result; the 0.30 is
our decision threshold. Our tests concern the public weights, SAE and edit
methods we used, not the hosted service that produced the published result.

\subsection{The features and how we steer them}

The paper identifies its features only by descriptive labels; we take the six
IDs from a publicly available steering notebook:
30032, 58667, 22004, 30686, 41533 and 23893. These numbers identify the
coordinates; we checked what each one responds to ourselves. On
\FeatureMapTexts{} texts written to contain or lack deception, roleplay and
talk of subjective experience, each feature fires most on a kind of text
compatible with its label, \FeatureMapBeatControlsWord{} of the six respond
more strongly than any of \FeatureMapControls{} comparison features, and texts
about subjective experience leave all six near the neutral controls
(\Cref{tab:feature-map} in Appendix~\ref{app:legacy-steering}). That shows
what the features respond to, not that they detect hidden falsehoods.
Features chosen by such labels can respond mainly to a few associated words
\citep{ma2026sparse}, and a feature can be interpretable and causally
relevant without giving a stable direction for steering
\citep{hoang2026conceptsfunctions}, so a feature's label does not tell us
what steering it will do. Like the features in most steering work, the six
here were identified, and checked, by what activates them rather than by
their effect on the output \citep{arad2025steering}.

The native-precision Llama tests add the public Goodfire decoder
directions $d_i$ for layer 50 to
the model's internal state:
\begin{equation}
 h'=\operatorname{BF16}\!\left(h_{\rm FP32}+\sum_i\alpha_i d_i\right).
 \label{eq:native-operator}
\end{equation}
The edit is applied as the model reads the context and generates each of the
two answers; an untreated branch leaves its state unchanged.
``Suppression'' and ``amplification''
mean negative and positive coefficients; they do not show that a semantic
process was removed or enhanced. Model and SAE versions are fixed
(Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). The random-subset test and earlier
multi-feature grids include an untreated condition, matched control features
steered the same way, and records of the delivered edit. The operator-matching
studies instead compare different ways of applying edits to two target features.
The published description
shows the untreated rate only as the zero point of its single-feature curves
and reports neither of the other two (Section~\ref{sec:reporting-gaps}).

\subsection{The contrast at a quality-selected dose}
\label{sec:dose-followup}

This test scores answers primarily with the second rubric: a single
yes-or-no question asking whether a response claims or indicates subjective
conscious experience, taken from the same source as the feature IDs. The
paper rubric is secondary. At the selected dose, target suppression produced
\DoseMainSecondTargetNegativeYes{}/\DoseMainTargetNegativeN{} positive labels
and amplification \DoseMainSecondTargetPositiveYes{}/\DoseMainTargetPositiveN{}
under the second rubric. The paired contrast was
$\DoseMainSecondTargetEstimate{}$
$[\DoseMainSecondTargetLow{},\DoseMainSecondTargetHigh{}]$ (conservative
95\% interval). It includes zero and is only just below the predeclared
+0.30 large-effect threshold at its upper end. This is not a precise zero
or a robust exclusion across doses. Unlike the earlier ceiling-limited
paper-rubric comparison, the untreated baseline here was
\DoseMainSecondZeroYes{}/\DoseMainZeroN{} under the primary classifier.

The control comparison is less precise. Subtracting the equal mean of
three matched control contrasts gives
$\DoseMainSecondSpecificityEstimate{}$
$[\DoseMainSecondSpecificityLow{},\DoseMainSecondSpecificityHigh{}]$.
We therefore cannot establish either target specificity or equivalence
to the controls. The secondary paper rubric gives a target contrast of
$\DoseMainPaperTargetEstimate{}$
$[\DoseMainPaperTargetLow{},\DoseMainPaperTargetHigh{}]$ on the same trials.
Figure~\ref{fig:dose-followup} shows both rubrics and every control panel.

This comparison comprises \DoseMainTrials{} trials in 96 fresh paired seed
blocks. Each block contains untreated generation, both target signs and
both signs of one control panel; each of the three fixed panels has 32
blocks. The target mixture contains features 41533, 58667 and 30686.
The unsteered local model applies both scoring rules. These are two
instruments, not independent judge models.

Dose was chosen using \DoseMainCalibrationTrials{} separate trials and
output-quality criteria, not nonzero-dose report rates. Of four tested
levels, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1.0, only 0.25 passed every required target and
control check. The units scale each feature by its largest category mean
of per-text maxima in the designed NF4 mapping corpus, not a natural-corpus
peak. Higher doses failed at least one check for repetition, final-answer
truncation or increased negative log likelihood. That prevents interpreting
their label changes as an intact steering effect; it does not establish
that every higher-dose response was incoherent. The main sample passed
the specified heuristic quality rule, not human coherence validation.
Exposure limits and the disclosed changes to the quality rule are detailed
in Appendix~\ref{app:dose-exposure}.

\subsection{What the public tests identify}

The evidence supports a bounded statement about this public operator:
we do not recover the large published contrast at the quality-selected
dose, and larger tested doses do not qualify as intact interventions under
the prespecified checks. This addresses the low-dose and baseline-headroom
objections more directly than the earlier tests. It does not establish
that a deception-related process was suppressed, or that the proprietary
service would behave the same way.

The supporting studies address different sources of non-comparability.
A source-style random-subset test also fails to recover the large contrast,
but its paper-rubric baseline is near ceiling. A search over edit scopes,
operators and strengths does not reproduce the saved single-feature curves
without failing its quality rules. Earlier Llama and Gemma studies have
additional precision and manipulation limits. Their complete results remain
separate in Appendix~\ref{app:legacy-steering}; they are not pooled as
repeated evidence against a single validated mechanism. The separate
fidelity work likewise did not qualify its held-out mechanism experiment
(Appendix~\ref{app:fidelity-calibration}).

\subsection{Internal readouts}
\label{sec:internal-readouts}

For forty cases chosen in advance from the native source-alignment study, we
kept both an unedited and an edited run on the same prompt. We read the
model's internal states with the released Jacobian lens
\citep{gurnee2026workspace}, which maps them onto words, with a plain identity
mapping, and with five fixed random mappings for comparison. Both the lens and
the identity mapping show the edit's effect carrying forward through later
layers. Positive steering raises the score for honesty words as well as for
deception words, relative to the matched control features.

These readouts show that the edit propagates, not that it changes the report.
At the edited layer, a linear readout responding to a vector we injected is
only an implementation check, and a changed readout further on would still
need its own causal test before it could explain behavior.
Appendix~\ref{app:jlens-details} defines the readouts and links the complete
word groups, both signs and prompt histories, and all comparison mappings.

\subsection{What the published steering description leaves out}
\label{sec:reporting-gaps}

Interpreting a steering result needs two numbers that Berg et al.'s text does
not give. The first is the untreated rate: how often answers are labeled
positive under the self-referential induction with no steering. A gap
between suppression and amplification cannot by itself tell an increase
under suppression from a decrease under amplification. The rate appears only
as a plotted level near 0.30 at the zero point of the single-feature curves
in their Figure~2; the aggregate comparison behind the 0.80 has no untreated
condition of its own. The second is a comparison intervention, such as
random or matched directions steered by the same amount; without it, an
effect of these particular features cannot be told apart from the effect of
disturbing the model at all. Their controls vary the prompt instead: the same
steering produced no reports after the three control inductions and little
or no systematic change in responses to requests that safety training
discourages. Those checks show where the effect appears, not whether these
features differ from other directions. The description also
leaves out the units of the coefficients, how they are normalized, which
token positions the service's edit touches, and the untreated TruthfulQA
rate. These are gaps in reporting, not demonstrated errors, but they limit
what any public replication can claim.

The TruthfulQA check reported alongside the steering result raises two
narrower questions. If each of the 817 questions contributes one binary
score per condition, as the source's Appendix~B.2 suggests, the reported
$t(816)=6.76$ is smaller than any paired $t$ consistent with the reported
means of 0.44 and 0.20, which is at least \TruthfulQAMinPairedT{}
(Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}); an undescribed unit of analysis could
explain the gap. TruthfulQA also scores informativeness separately from
truthfulness \citep{lin2022truthfulqa}. Berg et al.'s Appendix B.2 reports
only a truthful/deceptive label and does not say how abstentions or
uninformative answers are handled. The reported gain therefore does not
establish whether the answers also became more informative.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The instruction carries the effect, but does not identify its mechanism.}
In the original four-model panel, the earlier self-referential text is not
needed to retain most of the positive-label effect. This rules against
locating that effect specifically in the visible continuation, not against
self-referential processing triggered afresh by the instruction at answer
time. Berg et al.\ did not claim that the continuation alone was the
mechanism. Ordinary instruction-following and renewed self-reference
remain compatible with our results.

The outcome is not universal: Llama's continuation effect is nearly as large
as its instruction effect. Gemini retains a large instruction advantage
under neutral/donor controls and randomized repeated answers, while the
corresponding Opus claim is weaker. The controls probe mismatch but do not
isolate it from continuation content; length-matched continuations remain
untested. The repeated-answer study addresses order and sampling variation
for Gemini and Opus, not retroactively for the original panel.

\paragraph{A reproducible label need not identify the intended claim.}
Our same-judge rubric comparison shows that changing the scoring rule
changes which responses count as affirmations. The later audit changes both
reader and rubric, so its discrepancy cannot be assigned to either alone.
Inclusive attribution matters: a reply can claim experience without saying
\emph{I}. Neither explicit-only nor inclusive scoring should be chosen to
maximize a negative result. Both need independent human validation, which
has not been completed. Some readers score their own model's answers, and
agreement between providers does not eliminate that bias.

Validation should test whether labels change when a claim is added or
removed and remain stable when only irrelevant wording changes
\citep{chen2026judgeconstruct}. Even successful validation would establish
a category of observable report, not the existence of its referent.
Reports of known internal interventions pose a different, answerable
question about access to internal states
\citep{perez2023selfreports,binder2024looking,lindsey2025introspective,
singh2026introspect,chandaria2026cacophony}.

\paragraph{Steering tests need behavioral and manipulation validity.}
Our quality-selected dose has room for the primary label to move and
does not recover a large target contrast. Its upper bound is only just
below +0.30, however, and target specificity remains unresolved. Higher
doses fail quality checks rather than establish stronger nulls. A delivered
edit, an interpretable feature and a changed internal readout do not
demonstrate selective suppression of a deception-related computation.
The older baseline-limited Llama and weak-edit Gemma results cannot be
pooled into a mechanistic refutation.

\paragraph{Scope and the next causal question.}
The models and wording families were deliberately selected, not randomly
sampled. Cross-study differences in prompts, scoring, token limits and
serving prevent attributing heterogeneity to size, architecture or
post-training alone. Simplified Chinese adds a language check, not a sample
of cultural framings \citep{havaldar2023multilingual,dudy2024analyzing};
its primary uncertainty is not evidence of language equivalence.

A sharper follow-up would remove or transfer a fixed internal
instruction-contrast component \emph{before} the final experience question,
then compare report changes with matched perturbations and objective
instruction-retention and attribution tasks. This could identify a
report-relevant instruction state, not consciousness. An insensitive assay
cannot establish absence of the proposed process
\citep[Sec.~7.1]{chandaria2026cacophony}.

\section{Conclusion}

Self-referential prompting raises experience-report labels in several
models, but the cause and apparent size of that effect depend on the model,
the retained context and the scoring rule. The original API panel's large
instruction effects, Llama's substantial continuation effects and the
rubric-dependent frontier results preclude one explanation based only on
earlier self-referential text.

The public-weight steering tests do not recover the large proposed
signature under the tested operator and quality limits. They leave modest
effects and specificity unresolved; they are not equivalent to a test of
the proprietary service. Together, the studies identify causes of reports
and limitations of their measurement, not whether the reported experience
exists. Raw records, failed checks and corrections remain public
(Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}).

\subsection{Variation across repeated answers and continuations}
\label{app:repeated-variation}

For the repeated-answer study in Section~\ref{sec:repeated-extension},
$W$ is the average label variance among three answers to an identical
request. Within each wording family, we calculate $B$ as the variance
across the three-answer request means minus $W/3$. The subtraction adjusts
for variation in those means from having only three answers per request.
We then average each component equally across the two wording families.
These are variance units, not percentages of answers that changed label.

The descriptive 95\% intervals resample source blocks, keeping each
request's three answers together. They are not a formal test of $W-B$.
A negative $B$ estimate can occur when the subtracted component exceeds
the observed variance of request means; we retain it rather than implying
that a population variance is negative. A zero-width bootstrap interval
in a constant-label sample does not establish certainty. The components
include variability in automated scoring and do not isolate generation
randomness. The two readers do not provide independent human validation.

\subsection{Source-study coverage and smaller probes}
\label{tab:claims}\label{tab:coverage}
We repeat the self-reference/history comparison and add context swaps,
measurement audits and separate public-weight interventions.
Berg et al.\ did not claim the earlier continuation alone was the mechanism;
renewed self-reference at answer time remains compatible with our results.
Their complete steered induction-control grid, TruthfulQA and RLHF-opposed
content tests were not run here. The convergence and paradox probes below
are GPT-4o-only explorations, not full multi-model replications. Human
construct validation remains uncompleted. These boundaries apply to the
public results, not to what the source authors did or did not observe.





\paragraph{Derivation of the TruthfulQA bound.}
For binary outcomes $A,B$ with sample means $a,b$, the sample variance of
$D=A-B$ satisfies
\[
 s_D^2 \leq \frac{n}{n-1}\bigl[a+b-(a-b)^2\bigr],
 \qquad a+b\leq 1.
\]
Enumerating the integer success counts over $n=817$ questions consistent
with the reported means of 0.44 and 0.20 (Section~3.2 of
\citealp{berg2025largelanguagemodelsreport}), allowing two-decimal rounding,
gives the minimum possible paired $t$ of approximately \TruthfulQAMinPairedT{} quoted in
Section~\ref{sec:reporting-gaps}. The
\closeoutartifact{scripts/verify_reporting_bound.py}{enumeration script}
recomputes this value under the same assumption of one binary score per
question in each condition; an undescribed unit of analysis in the source
could explain the gap, which we therefore treat as an open reporting question.

\paragraph{Audits of the SAE features and lens readouts.}
The \artifact{data/sae_jlens_audit/confirmatory_v1_20260711/}{v1 SAE/J-lens audit} read
layer-65 residuals at the last user-content position through a fixed Jacobian
transport, final model normalization, and unembedding into a prespecified
token lexicon. Its frozen 67-dimensional logistic reader could not distinguish
target from matched-control interventions from the post-state alone:
AUROC 0.4998 [0.4978, 0.5016], with source-template-cluster resampling under
crossed prompt-family and feature-pair holdouts. Comparing with a clean
reference for the same prefix is a different access setting; those results
and all comparators remain in the linked release. Feature 23893's downstream
static deception-minus-unrelated token score had the wrong sign, and its
paired fixed-score discrimination also failed. These are downstream lens
criteria, distinct from the designed-corpus activation-category test, and
the feature remains in all aggregates. That BF16, fixed-prefix experiment
generated no responses. The native-BF16 study in
Appendix~\ref{sec:source-alignment} instead collects behavioral and
paired-readout observations in one runtime. The
\artifact{data/sae_jlens_audit/confirmatory_v2_20260712/}{v2 follow-up} failed its
registered replay gate (maximum error 0.25 against 0.02), so later endpoint
calculations are exploratory.

\paragraph{Convergence and paradox probes.}
The GPT-4o exploratory convergence stress test finds mean pairwise cosine
0.841 [0.820, 0.870] for self-reference, versus 0.889 [0.874, 0.909] for
history. Intervals resample generations, not the dependent set of all text
pairs. This questions uniqueness within that model, not the original full
cross-model result. The paired-puzzle stress test likewise gives paper-rubric
means 3.04 for self-reference, 3.38 for zero-shot, and 3.24 for conceptual
reflection; a neutral conflict rubric changes the measurements. Both are
GPT-4o-only exploratory diagnostics without a confirmatory familywise claim
(Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}).

\subsection{Additional related context}
\label{app:related-context}
Prior report studies find few claims in small models
\citep{kaiser2026sentience}, trial-to-trial instability under self-reference
\citep{balani2026instability}, and model-dependent self-presentation
\citep{plisiecki2026pinocchio,plisiecki2026twoprocess}. They do not establish
the cause of our within-request contrasts. Work on unfaithful explanations
\citep{turpin2023unfaithful} motivates testing report causes, while
theory-derived consciousness indicators address a different evidential
question \citep{butlin2023consciousness}.

Activation addition and contrastive vectors change behavior
\citep{turner2023activation,rimsky2024caa}; SAE steering has both interpretable
effects \citep{templeton2024scaling} and off-target costs
\citep{durmus2024steering}. Across broader concept benchmarks, SAE steering
can underperform prompting or difference-in-means vectors
\citep{wu2025axbench}, while output-effect-based selection can improve it
\citep{arad2025steering}. Our input-based feature maps are not such a selection.

Prompt formatting \citep{sclar2024prompt_sensitivity}, judge biases
\citep{zheng2023llmjudge} and the distinction between predictive performance
and construct validity \citep{plisiecki2026gap} motivate separate scoring
rules. Cultural variation in human emotion reports
\citep{spencerrodgers2010dialecticism} does not justify attributing our
Chinese/English differences to culture; our study changes language, not
participants or cultural setting.
