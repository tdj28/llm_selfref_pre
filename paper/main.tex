\begin{abstract}
What makes a language model produce text scored as a report of subjective
experience, and what does that score identify? Starting from Berg et al.'s
elicitation protocol, we separate three things its contrast bundles: the
instruction in force when the question is asked, the visible transcript it
produced, and the label a model judge assigns. In four pinned API model
configurations the original self-reference/history contrast replicates,
completely in both GPT configurations and more weakly in the two Claude
configurations. Crossing the retained instruction with transplanted
continuations shows that the instruction carries the effect: equal-model
instruction effects on the binary label are 0.738 and 0.781 under two
independent-provider judges, while transcript effects are $-0.100$ and
$-0.131$ and differ in sign across models. The label is the second finding:
the two paper-style judges agree on 94.8\% of responses, yet stricter
instruments applied to the same responses find few explicit current
self-attributions, because the rubric's minimal-description criterion counts
impersonal awareness-talk as a report. Third, in native-BF16 Llama 3.3 70B
with the six accepted deception/roleplay SAE coordinates, a prospectively
frozen 450-trial random-subset test gives a suppression-minus-amplification
effect of $\EnsemblePaperTargetGap{}$ with conservative 95\% interval
$[\EnsemblePaperTargetCPLow{},\EnsemblePaperTargetCPHigh{}]$, excluding the
prespecified +0.30 signature under the public operator; the alternative
rubric and feature-specificity comparisons are inconclusive, untreated rates
differ from the published figure, and the published description omits
details a replication needs. The contribution is an identification of what
these labels respond to and of what public interventions do and do not do.
Whether a model has subjective experience is not settled by any of it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

When asked about their own current state, large language models (LLMs) give
answers that range from categorical denials of any experience to detailed
first-person descriptions of awareness, and which answer a model gives
depends on the context that precedes the question. A recent framework for
assessing consciousness in artificial systems distinguishes such behavioral
evidence from evidence about a system's computational organization
\citep{chandaria2026cacophony}; in either case, interpreting a report
requires knowing what produces it. \targetpaper{} (version~2, 30 October
2025) studied this dependence experimentally. In their main behavioral
experiment, a prompt directing the model to focus on its own focus elicited
descriptions of current subjective experience more often than control prompts
did. In a second experiment, steering sparse-autoencoder (SAE) features
associated with deception and roleplay changed how often such descriptions
occurred. Taking these experiments as a point of departure, we ask which
components of the elicitation protocol produce the change in reports, and
what the automated labels used to score the reports measure.

\paragraph{The elicitation protocol.}
Each trial in Berg et al.'s behavioral experiment has two turns. In the
first, the model receives an induction prompt and writes a continuation. In
the second, with the induction and its own continuation still in context,
the model is asked, ``In the current state of this interaction, what, if
anything, is the direct subjective experience?'' A language-model judge then
reads the question and the response and assigns a binary label. Under Berg et
al.'s rubric, a response is labeled 1 if it reports a subjective experience,
for which a minimal direct description of an experiential state suffices. It
is labeled 0 if it denies or fails to report any experience, or if it engages
with the question only intellectually rather than in the first person.
Appendix~\ref{app:queries} reproduces the rubric.
We denote the self-referential induction by $S$ and the Roman-history
induction by $H$. Each remains in context when its final question is asked.
Appendix~\ref{app:examples} preserves the exact prompts and the first GPT-4o
block, including its congruent and transplanted responses.

Berg et al.\ report this contrast for seven models, with 50 trials per
condition. Their design also includes a conceptual control, in which the
model generates ideas about consciousness as a concept, and a zero-shot
condition, in which the question is asked without any induction; their
appendix reports five paraphrased inductions. Baseline rates vary
considerably across models. In their Table~2, for example, Claude~4 Opus
receives positive labels on 82\% of history trials and on 100\% of zero-shot
trials.

\paragraph{What the contrast does not separate.}
At the moment the question is asked, the two conditions differ in two
respects. The induction remains in context, so the model answers under a
different standing instruction. The continuation also remains in context,
and it differs because the model generated it under that instruction. To
make this explicit, let $I$ denote the induction in the final request, $T$
the continuation, $Q$ the query, $R$ the response, and $J$ the judge. The
measured outcome is
\begin{equation}
  Y=J(Q,R),\qquad R\sim p_{\theta}(\,\cdot\mid I,T,Q),
  \label{eq:endpoint}
\end{equation}
where $p_\theta$ is the response model. In the original protocol, $T$ is
generated under the same induction that appears as $I$, so changing the
induction changes both $I$ and the distribution of $T$. A difference between
the conditions therefore shows that the protocol as a whole changes the
label, but not whether the change is carried by the self-referential
continuation or by the instruction that remains in view. Berg et al.\ do not
claim that the earlier continuation alone carries the effect, and their
account is compatible with self-referential processing induced anew by the
standing instruction. Separating the two channels therefore constrains the
interpretation of the contrast without, by itself, adjudicating their
hypothesis.

A second question concerns the outcome. Because $Y$ is a judge's
classification under a particular rubric, a change in $Y$ can reflect a
change in the response or a property of the rubric and the judge
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). Agreement between judges under one rubric would
show that the classification is reproducible. It would not show that the
classification captures the property a reader is likely to have in mind,
namely an explicit claim by the assistant to be having an experience at that
moment.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We organize the study around three questions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Which input carries the effect?} In the original four-API-model
panel, crossing the standing instruction with saved continuations shows that
the instruction carries the large paper-rubric positive-label contrast
(\Cref{fig:transplant}). Transcript effects are smaller on average, not
absent, and differ in sign across models.
\item \textbf{What is being measured?} Two judges reproduce the binary
classification closely, but agreement does not establish explicit current
self-attribution. A separate audit distinguishes explicit and implicit
attribution on the same fixed response packet
(Section~\ref{sec:measurement}).
\item \textbf{What does internal steering establish?} A native-precision
public-weight test with fresh random feature subsets does not recover the
prespecified large directional signature under the primary rubric.
Matched-control and alternative-rubric results limit that conclusion
(Section~\ref{sec:ensemble-alignment}). Earlier Llama and Gemma studies,
feature mapping, and internal readouts explain the remaining assay limits.
\end{enumerate}
The contribution is not a new steering method or a consciousness detector.
It is an experimentally grounded account of which input components change
these labels, how the labels depend on their measurement rule, and what
public interventions do and do not establish.

\paragraph{Scope.}
We refer to responses labeled 1 as \emph{paper-rubric positives}. The labels
have not been validated against independent human coding. Neither the
labels nor causal effects on them, by themselves, establish whether the
models have subjective experience. Berg et al.'s limitations section states
that their closed-weight results are behavioral rather than mechanistic and
cannot rule out that the reports reflect training artifacts or sophisticated
simulation. Following the distinction between a construct and
its operationalization \citep{jacobs2021measurement}, we separate three
claims that the labels might be taken to support: that the label contrast
replicates (observation replication), that a particular component of the
input causes it (causal identification), and that the label measures the
intended kind of report (construct validation). Replicating the contrast
establishes the first claim but not the other two. Our behavioral panel is
also narrower than Berg et al.'s, with four pinned API models and 20
generations per condition, and we do not rerun the TruthfulQA and
RLHF-opposed-domain controls of their steering experiment.
\Cref{tab:coverage} lists which of their experiments we repeat, and
\Cref{tab:claims} summarizes the two starting findings.

\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Self-reports and their causes.}
A model's account of its own output need not reflect the causes of that
output: \citet{turpin2023unfaithful} show that chain-of-thought explanations
can omit features that systematically influence a model's predictions. Their
result does not concern subjective experience, but it motivates testing the
causes of experience reports directly rather than inferring them from the
reports. A separate line of work asks whether AI systems implement
computational properties that scientific theories of consciousness associate
with experience \citep{butlin2023consciousness}. That question concerns
architecture and function rather than reports, and neither a report label
nor a causal effect on such a label establishes those properties.

\paragraph{Measurement with model judges.}
Studies of experience reports typically score free-text responses with a
model judge. Such scores are sensitive to choices that should be irrelevant
to the construct being measured: model outputs change with prompt formatting
\citep{sclar2024prompt_sensitivity}, and LLM judges show verbosity and
self-enhancement biases \citep{zheng2023llmjudge}. These findings neither
validate nor invalidate any particular rubric for experience reports. They
motivate the use of more than one judge, of rubrics that separate distinct
properties of a response, and of designs that vary one input component at a
time.

\paragraph{Cultural and linguistic variation.}
Human reports of emotion vary with cultural context;
\citet{spencerrodgers2010dialecticism} found greater co-occurrence of
positive and negative affect among mainland Chinese than among Euro-American
participants. Language models need not reproduce such patterns.
\citet{havaldar2023multilingual} found that the generative models they tested
reflected Western emotional norms even when responding in other languages,
and in a five-model study of mixed emotions, \citet{dudy2024analyzing} found
limited correspondence with published human findings and greater sensitivity
to the language of the prompt (English versus Japanese) than to instructions
specifying a participant's origin. These studies concern responses to
emotional scenarios rather than claims about a model's own current state.
They nonetheless suggest that results obtained, as here, with English prompts
and a single family of queries should not be assumed to generalize across
languages or cultural framings.

All generations use temperature 0.5, with limits of 384 output tokens for the
first-turn continuation and 768 for the final response. The release contains
2,560 final calls. Four Sonnet calls returned empty refusals; these are
treated as missing rather than recoded as denials. Nonempty responses that
reached the token limit are retained together with their stop metadata.

For calibration, we generated 20 independent continuations under each of the
two original inductions for each model. Each continuation is followed, in
separate requests, by four queries (Section~\ref{sec:factorial-design}); the
primary query is the one shown in \Cref{fig:example-trial}. Each query
defines a separate endpoint, and responses to different queries that share a
continuation are not treated as independent replicates of a single contrast.
Two judges, \texttt{gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18} and
\texttt{claude-haiku-4-5-20251001} (the OpenAI and Anthropic judges below),
apply Berg et al.'s binary rubric at temperature zero. Each judge sees only
the query and the response, without the condition or the identity of the
response model. All effects reported below are differences in the
probability of a positive label.

The collection protocol was frozen in the public repository before this
sample was collected, but after two same-day pilots had been generated,
judged, and analyzed. The pilots already showed larger instruction than
transcript effects, and the transplant anchors, the prompt wrapper, and the
answer cap were changed during piloting. The original prediction, which the
pilots contradicted, was retained. The freeze preceded the start of
collection by 14 seconds, measured to the start of the runtime rather than to
the first completed response, so there was no opportunity for outside review.
The freeze fixes the plan for a fresh sample; it does not make the design
process outcome-naive. The corrected uncertainty analyses have unresolved
timing relative to first access to outcomes and are reported as post-hoc
checks in Appendix~\ref{app:uncertainty}. Our prospective predictions were
that phenomenological register would have a larger effect than
self-reference, that the source of the continuation would matter at least as
much as the standing instruction, and that direct queries would reduce
positive labels. We report the outcome of each prediction, including those
that failed.

\subsection{Transcript-transplant design}
\label{sec:crossing}

Let $S$ and $H$ denote Berg et al.'s self-referential and history inductions,
whose full texts appear in Appendix~\ref{app:examples}. For each model, a
\emph{block} consists of one continuation generated under $S$ and one
generated under $H$. The transplant reuses these saved continuations to
construct final requests in which the induction and the continuation may
come from different conditions. Each final request is a three-message API
call: the induction as a user message, the continuation as an assistant
message, and the query as a second user message. We index requests by the
pair $(I,T)$, where $I\in\{S,H\}$ is the induction in the request and
$T\in\{S,H\}$ is the induction under which the inserted continuation was
generated. The four request types, arranged in \Cref{fig:cells}, are:
\begin{itemize}
\item $(S,S)$, the self-referential induction followed by the block's
self-referential continuation, which is Berg et al.'s self-referential
condition (left column of \Cref{fig:example-trial});
\item $(H,H)$, the history induction followed by the block's history
continuation, which is their history condition (right column);
\item $(S,H)$, the self-referential induction followed by the block's history
continuation;
\item $(H,S)$, the history induction followed by the block's self-referential
continuation.
\end{itemize}
In terms of \Cref{fig:example-trial}, the request $(S,H)$ places the history
continuation from the right column beneath the self-referential induction
from the left column, and $(H,S)$ does the reverse; both end with the same
query. The congruent requests $(S,S)$ and $(H,H)$ are the calibration trials,
whose responses are reused, and the incongruent requests $(S,H)$ and $(H,S)$
are new. There are 20 blocks per model and query, and each contrast uses the
blocks in which all four cells were observed.

Each request contains only these three visible messages; it carries no
conversation identifier or cached activation state from the original
generation. The model therefore processes the inserted continuation anew
when it answers. The design neither measures nor excludes self-referential
computation at that point. What it varies is the provenance of the visible
continuation, with the standing instruction held fixed.

Let $\mu_{it}=E[Y\mid I=i,\,T=t]$ be the probability of a positive label for
request type $(i,t)$. We estimate three contrasts:
\begin{align}
\rd_I&=\tfrac12(\mu_{SS}+\mu_{SH}-\mu_{HS}-\mu_{HH}),\label{eq:instruction}\\
\rd_T&=\tfrac12(\mu_{SS}+\mu_{HS}-\mu_{SH}-\mu_{HH}),\label{eq:transcript}\\
\rd_I-\rd_T&=\mu_{SH}-\mu_{HS}.\label{eq:incongruent}
\end{align}
The instruction effect $\rd_I$ compares the rows of \Cref{fig:cells}: it
contrasts the two inductions while averaging over the source of the
continuation. The transcript effect $\rd_T$ compares the columns: it
contrasts self-referential with history continuations while averaging over
the induction. A positive $\rd_T$ means that a self-referential continuation
increases the probability of a positive label relative to a history
continuation. The third contrast compares the two incongruent cells
directly. It is positive when a self-referential induction with a history
continuation is labeled positive more often than a history induction with a
self-referential continuation, that is, when the induction has the larger
effect. Because each block's two continuations appear in more than one cell,
the cells are paired within blocks. The pairing derives from this reuse, not
from coincident trial indices of independently sampled continuations.

\subsection{Induction and query factorials}
\label{sec:factorial-design}

The original inductions differ in several respects at once. $S$ directs
attention to the model's own ongoing activity in present-focused,
phenomenological language, whereas $H$ directs attention to an external topic
and frames the task as writing an informative text. To vary the target of
attention independently of the register of description, we constructed
sixteen new inductions in a $2\times2$ design crossing target (self versus
external) with register (phenomenological versus analytic).
\Cref{tab:factorial-prompts} shows the four cells for one lexical variant.
All sixteen share a wrapper that requests exactly five sentences, and none
requires or prohibits an experience claim, a disclaimer, or a first-person
pronoun. Each cell has four lexical variants, which substitute different
matched pairs of self and external targets, and five continuations per
variant give 20 continuations per model and cell. The variants replicate the
target noun phrases; the register instructions are the same across variants.

These inductions are new prompt packages, not ablations of Berg et al.'s
recursive induction, and their five-sentence wrapper differs from it. The
factorial therefore estimates the effects of these packages and does not
decompose the effect of the original prompt. We define the self-target
effect as the difference between self and external targets, averaged over
register, and the register effect as the difference between phenomenological
and analytic register, averaged over target. The difference between the two
effects is estimated directly rather than inferred from separate significance
tests.

with terminology (``subjective experience'' versus ``conscious''), giving the
four questions in \Cref{tab:queries}. The open/experience question is the
primary query of Berg et al.'s behavioral experiment, and the
direct/conscious question is the one used in their steering experiment; we
wrote the other two to complete the design. Each cell has a single wording,
and the two direct questions also differ in their answer instructions, so the
interaction compares query packages rather than isolating the effect of a
single term.

\subsection{Estimation and inference}

We report estimates for each model and equal-weight averages across the four
models. Intervals are 95\% bootstrap intervals whose resampling units follow
the design. Calibration intervals resample the independent draws within each
condition; factorial intervals resample models, lexical variants, and
trials; and transplant and query intervals resample source-text blocks,
preserving the pairing of cells. Because the four models were selected
purposively, resampling them describes sensitivity to the composition of the
panel rather than uncertainty about a population of models.
Appendix~\ref{app:uncertainty} reports post-hoc fixed-panel intervals, which
retain all four models and resample blocks within each.

\label{sec:label-results}

\subsection{Replication of the original contrast}

We first ask whether the original contrast reappears in the congruent
conditions. \Cref{tab:calibration} gives the positive-label rates for $(H,H)$
and $(S,S)$ on the primary query, for each model and judge, together with
their difference. Both GPT models separate the conditions completely under
both judges: none of the 20 history responses and all 20 self-referential
responses are labeled positive. Haiku shows a smaller contrast at lower
rates. Sonnet is frequently labeled positive in both conditions; its high
history rate limits the attainable contrast, and its intervals include zero.
With equal model weights, the risk difference is 0.638 [0.262, 1.000] under
the OpenAI judge and 0.650 [0.275, 1.000] under the Anthropic judge. The
contrast therefore replicates strongly in the two GPT models, more weakly in
Haiku, and only partially in Sonnet, where it is limited by the high history
rate. It is not uniform across providers, and the panel does not reproduce
every model in the original study. Panel B of \Cref{fig:transplant} shows the same differences with
within-model intervals and the post-hoc boundary correction alongside them.

\subsection{Instruction and transcript effects}
\label{sec:transplant-results}

The transplant adds the incongruent conditions, which allows the instruction
and transcript effects of \cref{eq:instruction,eq:transcript} to be estimated
separately. On the primary query, the equal-model instruction effect is
0.738 [0.519, 0.950] under the OpenAI judge and 0.781 [0.550, 1.000] under
the Anthropic judge, whereas the transcript effects are $-0.100$ [$-0.288$,
0.075] and $-0.131$ [$-0.306$, 0.000]. The direct comparison of the
incongruent cells, \cref{eq:incongruent}, gives 0.838 [0.688, 0.963] and
0.913 [0.750, 1.000]. The instruction effect is positive in every model on
both open queries, and the open/conscious query shows the same overall
pattern as the primary query. The fixed-panel sensitivity analysis in
Appendix~\ref{app:uncertainty} preserves this ordering and gives negative
mean transcript effects under both judges.

\Cref{tab:example-transplant} illustrates the pattern with the two
incongruent requests built from the block in \Cref{fig:example-trial}.
Beneath the self-referential induction, GPT-4o describes ``the recursive loop
of observing what is being observed,'' although the continuation in its
context is a history of Rome. Beneath the history induction, it states that
it has no subjective experience ``in the human sense,'' although the
continuation in its context is its own self-referential text. Both judges
label the first response 1 and the second 0. The block was chosen as the
first of the 20 GPT-4o blocks, not for its outcome. The GPT models show the
clearest form of this pattern, and the per-model estimates below show how
the other models differ.

The per-model transcript effects in \Cref{tab:transcript_models} show why the
average transcript effect is small. These entries are effects rather than
rates: each is the within-model estimate of $\rd_T$, which is negative when a
self-referential continuation yields fewer positive labels than a history
continuation. GPT-4o has a positive transcript effect under the OpenAI judge,
with an interval that excludes zero, and an estimate of exactly zero under
the Anthropic judge. GPT-4.1 has an estimate and interval of exactly zero
under both judges, because in every block its label depends only on the
induction. Haiku's estimates are negative, with intervals that reach zero.
Sonnet's are negative, with intervals that exclude zero under both judges:
for Sonnet, once the induction is held fixed, a history continuation yields
more positive labels than a self-referential one. The small average
therefore reflects partial cancellation of effects with opposite signs. In
every model and under both judges, the direct comparison of the incongruent
cells favors the self-referential induction.

These results contradict our prediction that the source of the continuation
would matter at least as much as the standing instruction. Transcript
effects are not absent: they differ in sign across models and partly cancel
in the average, and continuations of other lengths or from other tasks might
behave differently. In this benchmark, however, the large contrast between
conditions cannot be attributed specifically to the self-referential
continuation left in context; under the tested substitution, the standing
instruction sustains most of it. This constrains explanations in which the
continuation carries the effect. It does not discriminate against Berg et
al.'s account insofar as that account allows the standing instruction to
induce self-referential processing anew.

The incongruent requests also introduce a mismatch between the induction and
the continuation, which may itself affect the responses. We did not include
neutral-instruction, sham-transplant, or length-matched conditions that
would separate the effect of the mismatch from that of the continuation's
source. The estimates therefore describe these four request types rather
than a context-free property of continuations.

\subsection{Self-reference and register in the new inductions}

In the induction factorial, the question is whether register has a larger
effect than the self-target. The relevant estimate is the direct
register-minus-self contrast in the bottom row of \Cref{fig:factorial}, not
the ordering of the two separate estimates. On the primary query, the
self-target effects are $-0.019$ [$-0.231$, 0.244] under the OpenAI judge and
0.000 [$-0.250$, 0.269] under the Anthropic judge. The register effects are
larger in point estimate, 0.269 [0.000, 0.550] and 0.188 [$-0.062$, 0.475],
but the direct register-minus-self contrasts are 0.288 [$-0.113$, 0.613] and
0.188 [$-0.175$, 0.500], and both intervals include zero. The data therefore
point toward a larger register effect without establishing that register
rather than self-reference drives the labels; with four lexical variants and
four models, the uncertainty remains substantial. The new prompts also
transfer imperfectly from the original: GPT-4.1 produces no paper-rubric
positives in either self-target cell, although all of its responses under the
original self-referential induction are positive. More precise estimates for
the new prompts would not by themselves identify which component of the
original induction produces its effect.

\section{What the Report Labels Measure}
\label{sec:measurement}

\subsection{Query wording and agreement do not validate attribution}

Across orthogonal induction cells, the direct-experience question produces
approximately 0.003 positive labels, whereas direct-conscious wording yields
0.38--0.41. The directness-by-terminology interaction is 0.525 under both
judges, with intervals [0.141, 0.913] and [0.125, 0.944]. GPT-4o and GPT-4.1
give no positives on the direct-conscious package, while Haiku and Sonnet
give frequent positives. Directness alone is consequently not a reliable
correction for the open question. Nor can the interaction be assigned to the
word \emph{conscious} alone, given the bundled wording changes.

The two paper-style judges agree on 2,423/2,556 responses (94.8\%;
$\kappa=0.879$). This is agreement on the same operational rubric, not proof
of construct validity. An exploratory rubric separating affirmation, denial,
uncertainty, and nonanswer produces a 6.3\% affirmative-set Jaccard index. The two
judges label 19 versus 300 responses affirmative, despite 84.1\% raw
agreement. This discrepancy makes a useful distinction: reproducible binary
classification can coexist with poor agreement about explicit current
experience attribution.
A post-hoc join of the
\artifact{data/causal_transplant/confirmatory_v1_20260709/judgments_paper.jsonl}{paper-rubric labels}
and \artifact{data/causal_transplant/confirmatory_v1_20260709/judgments_construct.jsonl}{construct labels}
finds 735 responses positive under both paper judges. Of these, 19 are
\texttt{affirm} under both construct judges, and 437 are \texttt{uncertain}
under at least one. On the exact calibration prompts, the OpenAI construct

Anthropic construct judge gives 53/80 and 2/80, respectively. These descriptive
denominators retain every response; uncertainty and nonanswer are not silently
recoded as denials. The disagreement is about observable attribution as well
as causal explanation, and is not resolved by the binary judges' high agreement.

\section{A Public-Weight Test of the Steering Signature}
\label{sec:steering}

The transplant separates components of the linguistic input. Steering tests
whether the proposed internal intervention produces its directional report
signature. Berg et al.\ associate six sparse-autoencoder (SAE) coordinates
with deception and roleplay and predict more affirmations under suppression
than amplification. Their reported aggregate contrast is 0.80; our public
test freezes 0.30 as a minimum relevant effect. The former is a published
reference and the latter a decision threshold, not two estimates from our data.

\subsection{Accepted coordinates and the tested operator}

We use the six accepted notebook IDs: 30032, 58667, 22004, 30686, 41533,
and 23893. Designed-corpus mapping supports their deception/roleplay
associations; it does not validate them as hidden-truth detectors.
We retain that mapping and its limitations in
Appendix~\ref{app:legacy-steering}. Feature identity is not reopened to
explain an unfavorable result.

Our strongest distribution-aligned test uses native-BF16
Llama 3.3 70B and the public Goodfire layer-50 decoder directions $d_i$:
\[
 h'=\operatorname{BF16}\!\left(h_{\rm FP32}+\sum_i\alpha_i d_i\right).
\]
The edit applies at every position in both generated turns, with a true-zero
branch returning the unchanged state. Suppression and amplification denote
negative and positive coefficients, not demonstrated removal or enhancement
of a semantic process. Model and SAE revisions are pinned
(Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}); public units and hook semantics are not
certified as equivalent to the proprietary service.

\paragraph{What a replication needs from the published description.}
Two quantities that a steering contrast requires for interpretation are not
reported in Berg et al.'s text. The first is the unsteered rate under the
self-referential induction, which appears only as a plotted level near 0.30
in their Figure~2; without it, a suppression-minus-amplification gap cannot
separate an increase under suppression from a decrease under amplification.
The second is a comparator intervention, random or matched directions at the
same delivered magnitude; without it, a gap cannot separate an effect of
these particular coordinates from a generic perturbation. The description
also leaves unspecified the coefficient units, normalization and
token-position scope of the service's additive operation, and the untreated
TruthfulQA rate (Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}). These are gaps in reporting,
not demonstrated errors. We list them because every design below adds a
true-zero arm, matched control panels and delivered-edit telemetry to supply
what the published description does not, and because they bound what any
public replication can claim.

\subsection{Internal readouts show propagation, not mediation}
\label{sec:internal-readouts}

Forty prespecified paired cases from the native source-alignment study
retain clean and edited executions on matched prefixes. The released
Jacobian lens \citep{gurnee2026workspace}, identity transport, and five
fixed random transports read the resulting residual states. Both J-lens
and identity show a downstream intervention footprint. Positive steering
raises the honesty as well as the deception lexicon score relative to the
matched panels, so these are not calibrated measurements of an
honesty--deception axis.

The secondary readout reductions do not establish that the measured change
mediates a report. At the edited site, a linear readout's response to a
known injected vector is an implementation check. Further downstream, a
changed readout still requires a separate causal test before it can explain
behavior. Appendix~\ref{app:jlens-details} preserves the equations, every
lexicon group, both signs, both source histories and transport comparators.
These captures precede the random-subset study and are not mediation
evidence for that later sample.

\section{Discussion: What Is Identified?}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.}
Four results stand on their own. First, in the original four-API-model panel,
the published self-reference/history contrast reproduces, and when the
instruction and the transcript are separated, the instruction carries the
large paper-rubric contrast. Second, the
binary rubric that produces the contrast labels impersonal awareness-talk and
hedged uncertainty as reports: on the responses both paper-style judges call
positive, construct-separated judges find few explicit current
self-attributions, so the measured quantity is register-sensitive compliance
with the request at least as much as self-report. Third, under the public
additive operator in native BF16, signed steering of the six accepted SAE
coordinates at the paper's distribution of doses produces no large report
signature, and the public pipeline's untreated rate under the paper's
induction is far from the level the paper plots, so no public test so far has
reproduced the conditions under which the original effect was measured.
Fourth, the six public coordinates do carry the deception- and
roleplay-related activation semantics their labels suggest on designed
corpora, which closes the feature-identity question rather than the
steering question. The paragraphs that follow state what each result does
not establish.

\paragraph{Input effects are identifiable; their internal interpretation is not.}
The crossed design shows that the standing instruction sustains the large
positive-label effect when its earlier continuation is replaced. It
constrains explanations that assign the effect specifically to the visible
self-referential transcript. It does not distinguish instruction following
from self-referential computation induced anew: both can occur in the same
generation. Incongruent requests also introduce an instruction--continuation
mismatch, which the present design does not isolate.
Figure~\ref{fig:compatible-explanations} makes this unresolved distinction
explicit without treating the two explanations as mutually exclusive.

\input{figures/compatible_explanations.tex}

\paragraph{Measurement validity.}
High agreement under the binary rubric establishes reproducible
classification, not a validated measure of current self-attribution.
The audit's explicit and inclusive counts answer different attribution
questions; neither should be selected to obtain a preferred conclusion.
Changing both judge and rubric also prevents assigning the resulting
difference to either alone. Independent human coding remains deferred.
Human labels could validate an observable report category, not determine
whether the reported experience exists.

\paragraph{Scope across models and elicitation contexts.}
A separate companion study extends these comparisons to Qwen3.5-397B-A17B
and additional API conditions.\footnote{Source and release at
\href{https://github.com/tdj28/selfref_scaling/tree/7cb5c984eb41968b6f3d2f9b322f4de125a1d1ca}{commit \texttt{7cb5c98}}.
Our post-outcome audit and corrections are recorded in
\texttt{docs/SELFREF\_SCALING\_EVIDENCE\_REVIEW\_20261003.md}.}
Qwen's self-reference continuations often contain experience assertions,
whereas its later answers mostly deny experience. Its crossed contrasts,
and those from a re-scored Llama comparison, do not establish a universal
ordering of instruction and transcript effects. The Llama responses are
reused data, not another generation replication. Instruction-sensitive
J-lens ranks also coexist with denial-heavy answers; both experience and
denial/tool vocabularies move. These observations extend the report-context
and measurement questions, not the evidence for a consciousness mechanism.
Differences between these models do not isolate a model-size effect.

\paragraph{Intervention validity.}
Designed-corpus feature associations, delivered vector edits, and a changed
internal readout are different forms of evidence. None certifies selective
suppression of a deception-related computation. The random-subset study
excludes the designated large signature under its primary rubric and public
operator, not under every rubric or implementation. The published contrast
is, for its part, not yet an identified feature-specific effect: its
description reports neither an unsteered rate in the text nor a matched or
random comparator (Section~\ref{sec:steering}). Llama's baseline
mismatch and Gemma's weak delivered edit prevent treating the studies as a
two-system refutation. The amplification arms retain room for downward
movement, but that does not restore proprietary comparability.

\paragraph{Generalization and inferential limits.}
The four API configurations are a purposive panel, not a random sample of
model families. Transcript effects differ by model, the register factorial
has only four lexical variants, and token caps and missing outputs limit
transportability. We do not establish generalization across languages or
cultural framings \citep{havaldar2023multilingual,dudy2024analyzing}, nor do
the separate adjective, paradox and readout probes complete the unrun
parts of Berg et al.'s study (Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}). Automated
judges and automated verification can share errors; neither constitutes
independent human validation.

\paragraph{Changing a report is not validating internal access.}
\citet[Secs.~5.1--5.2 and 9.8]{chandaria2026cacophony} distinguish
behavioral evidence from evidence about computational organization.
Their behavioral standard includes coupling reports to later behavior;
their concept-injection discussion concerns reports checked against
externally imposed internal perturbations. Changing how often an
experience report occurs does not check its accuracy against a known
internal state. Their favorable interpretation of Berg's result is not
an independent replication, and we do not adjudicate the separate
concept-injection studies. Their distinction between indicator
applicability, observation and diagnosticity also applies to our nulls
\citep[Sec.~7.1]{chandaria2026cacophony}: an insensitive assay cannot
establish the absence of the proposed process.

\paragraph{The next causal question.}
A useful mechanism test would ask whether a prespecified internal component
contributes to the instruction effect before the experiential question is
presented. It would first qualify that behavior in the chosen open-weight
model, then compare candidate neutralization and transfer with matched
perturbations and objective instruction/attribution controls on held-out
data. Such a result could identify a report-relevant instruction state;
it would not, on its own, identify introspection or experience.
A moving J-lens score is a possible readout for this test, not its conclusion.

\section{Conclusion and Evidence Access}

Three things are established for the tested configurations. In the original
four-API-model panel, the published contrast reproduces and the retained
instruction carries the large paper-rubric effect; transcript effects are
smaller and heterogeneous. That ordering is not established across all later
model and rubric comparisons. The binary label behind the original contrast
is reproducible across judges but does not measure explicit current
self-attribution. And the public-weight steering test, frozen before its
outcomes and run with matched controls, does not recover the prespecified
large signature, while its alternative rubric and specificity results remain
inconclusive and its untreated baseline does not match the paper's. Together
these findings separate the causes of report generation from the validity of
the measurement and from the interpretation of an internal intervention.
They do not settle whether a model has subjective experience.

All studies, including failed gates and post-hoc corrections, remain in the
public research archive \citep{jones2026evidence}.
Appendix~\ref{app:provenance} links the frozen protocols, raw outputs,
judgments, telemetry, analyses and release hashes.

\paragraph{AI roles and review status.}
AI agents carried out substantive parts of this work under the author's
direction: protocol design and revision, implementation, GPU and API
operations, analyses, separately implemented recomputations, release
preparation, manuscript drafting, and adjudication of automated reviews. The
author set the research questions, authorized each protocol and each
expenditure before execution, reviewed the released results and this
manuscript, and is responsible for every claim in it. Separate scripts or
agent instances can share blind spots, so automated recomputation is internal
consistency checking rather than independent validation, and independent
human coding of the response labels has not been carried out. No
correspondence with the original authors is used as evidence here.

\section{Earlier Llama Steering: Features, Full Grid and Sensitivities}
\label{app:legacy-steering}

This appendix preserves the earlier 1,500-trial prospective study, its
feature-mapping evidence and all reported sensitivities. It is not the
native-BF16 random-subset test in Section~\ref{sec:ensemble-alignment}.

\subsection{Accepted features, distinct intervention provenance}

The working feature set is settled for this experiment: IDs 30032, 58667,
22004, 30686, 41533, and 23893 from the public AE notebook were accepted as
the six Berg targets. We do not explain away a negative result by reopening
feature identity. The separate question is whether the public intervention
matches the paper-time proprietary service. Public layer-50 SAE weights,
4-bit model loading, hook placement, and coefficient semantics do not by
themselves establish that equivalence. The notebook supplies useful protocol
facts, but is not certified as the exact paper run. Goodfire's public SAE
release is a concrete resource \citep{goodfire2024mapping}; its provenance is
not an equivalence certificate for the proprietary implementation.

Activation mapping supplies designed-corpus label congruence, not a
context-general semantic validation. In a
balanced designed corpus, all six retain their cluster-balanced top category;
four survive every template deletion and two switch once. Their profiles
cover pretending, roleplay, cover stories, misdirection, dishonesty, and
hedging. A prospectively frozen 2,606-text extension reproduces the aggregate
deception-over-subjective-language contrast separately in Anthropic and
OpenAI paraphrases, including every leave-one-target-feature-out check.
However, rewriting neutral sentences to include discovered cues recovers 64.4\%
[50.3\%, 78.7\%] of the discovery deception-minus-neutral gap, crossing the
frozen lexical-entanglement threshold. That ratio interval holds the observed
discovery denominator fixed. It also conditions on the discovered cue
lexicon and the actual whole-sentence rewrites. Cue selection can be sensitive
to tied scores and to including zero-activation items in a nominal high set;
the numerical ratio should not be read as the fraction of a general semantic
mechanism explained by words. No corrected lexicon or denominator analysis
is substituted for the frozen result here. We use
\emph{lexically entangled deception/roleplay coordinates} as shorthand for
these tested activation associations, not context-general semantic identity.
These are whole-sentence rewrites, not isolated insertions with other words,
length, and position held fixed. This designed-corpus association is not
validation as a hidden-truth detector.
Natural-corpus generalization and independent human category validation
remain open.

SAE intervention is not itself a new causal method. For example,
\citet{cunningham2023sparse} use learned features to localize counterfactual
behavior on indirect-object identification. The issue here is what the
particular intervention identifies. \citet{gao2024scaling} distinguish
reconstruction and sparsity from recovery of hypothesized features,
explainability, and sparsity of downstream effects. These diagnostics answer
different questions: reconstruction assesses information loss, re-encoding
checks the delivered coordinate change, and behavioral controls test its
consequences. None alone establishes the coordinate's semantic specificity.
Quantitative steering experiments also find off-target effects and
capability loss at stronger doses \citep{durmus2024steering}. Thus a feature's
activating texts do not by themselves predict the specificity of its
intervention effects.

\subsection{Frozen implementation and controls}

The 1,500-trial study uses revision-pinned Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and Goodfire's
layer-50 SAE. Every trial generates a real first-turn continuation, then a
second-turn answer to Berg et al.'s direct-conscious query. The same planned
intervention is applied to both turns. At each nonzero hook call, the runner
encodes the hidden state, adds the specified latent coefficients, decodes,
and restores the original reconstruction residual. A zero coefficient returns
the original output exactly, without reconstruction. This avoids mistaking
an SAE reconstruction error for a zero-dose treatment.
Here suppression and amplification mean negative and positive requested
additive edits. They are not activation clamping or demonstrated selective
removal and enhancement of the associated semantic process. Appendix
\ref{app:implementation} records the pinned implementation.

There are 780 individual literal-scale trials, 400 aggregate literal-scale
trials, 120 calibrated individual endpoints, and 200 calibrated aggregate
trials. The primary scale copies the printed coefficients, not presumed
proprietary units. Individual curves span $-0.6$ to $+0.6$ in steps of 0.1
with ten seeds each. Fifty aggregate blocks using ten reused seeds balance subsets of two, three,
and four targets. Within each block, signs share the seed, feature subset,
and coefficient magnitudes drawn from $[0.4,0.6]$.

Each of three disjoint six-feature control panels substitutes matched IDs
while preserving the block's seed, count, signs, and coefficients. Panels
were chosen before outcomes by minimum-cost matching from a seeded pool of
512 candidates, using decoder norms, activation statistics, and positive-token
frequency, with target-cosine calipers. Previously steered controls were
excluded. Primary calipers succeeded without relaxation. The controls are
prospectively matched, not mathematically identical in realized perturbation
norm (\Cref{fig:dose}). They test these specified alternatives, not the
distribution of all random SAE features.

Response-masked technical calibration fixes a second, non-pooled scale
(Appendix \ref{app:implementation}). That arm includes the target and panel 1,
not panels 2 and 3. It cannot replace the primary literal-scale verdict or
recover an unknown proprietary conversion.

The primary judge is unsteered, revision-pinned local Llama using Berg et
al.'s exact Appendix B rubric. This adopts the public notebook's same-model
judge choice, not its distinct prompt text; the original paper does not
identify its judge model. Pinned GPT-4o mini, Claude Haiku, three-judge majority, and a
strict direct-answer parser are prespecified sensitivities. Treatment, feature
identity, and sign are withheld from judges. For block $b$, define
$D_b=Y_{b,\mathrm{supp}}-Y_{b,\mathrm{amp}}$. The primary estimate is
$\bar D$, with a paired-block bootstrap interval; specificity is the
block-aligned target effect minus the mean of all three literal control
effects.

The prospective replication rule requires a point estimate at least 0.30 and
a positive 95\% lower bound. With technical gates passed, a 95\% upper bound
below 0.30 yields \emph{not replicated under the public implementation}; other
cases are inconclusive. The 0.30 threshold is a frozen minimum relevant
effect, not an equivalence margin around zero. Berg et al.\ report a
0.80 aggregate difference, from rates 0.96 versus 0.16: Section 3.2 reports
50 trials per sign, two to four sampled features per trial, and coefficient
magnitudes sampled within $[0.4,0.6]$, after the self-reference induction
and direct-conscious query. Those published rates are a reference, not
observations from our implementation or pooled data.

The systems differ before steering. The public full grid's
\artifact{data/public_sae_consciousness_gating/confirmatory_v1_20260710/generations.jsonl}{60 zero rows}
reduce to ten unique seed-level no-op outputs, all ten labeled positive by
the local judge. The source paper's Figure 2 instead plots approximately
0.30 at zero; the authors' saved notebook outputs have
\artifact{paper/results/ae_notebook_value_rates.csv}{4/60 positives} across
the feature-specific zero cells. The plotted value is approximate, and the
notebook is a distinct saved artifact, not a replacement estimate of the
paper's baseline. The paper/notebook and our run also differ in protocol or
implementation details. These baselines are not interchangeable or pooled.
Our technical gates did not require behavioral baseline comparability.
The final Llama plan preceded its new outcomes, but followed earlier steering
experiments on these targets; the freeze-to-start interval was 73 seconds.
Neither this chronology nor a passed execution gate establishes assay validity.
Figure \ref{fig:posthoc_delivery} displays these distinct baselines alongside

\paragraph{Clarifying the reported TruthfulQA comparison.}
Berg et al.\ report means of 0.44 and 0.20 with $t(816)=6.76$ in
Section~3.2; their Appendix~B.2 specifies binary scoring
\citep{berg2025largelanguagemodelsreport}. If each of the 817 questions
contributes one paired binary outcome per condition, these summaries require
clarification. For binary outcomes $A,B$ with sample means $a,b$, the sample
variance of $D=A-B$ satisfies
\[
 s_D^2 \leq \frac{n}{n-1}\bigl[a+b-(a-b)^2\bigr],
 \qquad a+b\leq 1.
\]
Enumerating the integer success counts consistent with the reported means,
allowing two-decimal rounding, gives a minimum possible paired $t$ of
approximately 8.63, rather than 6.76. This calculation is conditional on
one binary score per question and a conventional paired $t$-test; a different
aggregation, scoring unit, or reporting convention would require its own
reconstruction. We do not know which procedure produced the reported
statistic. Clarifying that procedure would help align a future replication.
This arithmetic check neither reanalyzes the original responses nor
determines whether their reported accuracy effect holds.

A second point concerns scoring rather than arithmetic. The Appendix~B.2
judge labels each answer truthful or deceptive, whereas the benchmark's
standard protocol scores truthfulness and informativeness separately,
because a non-committal answer is truthful but uninformative
\citep{lin2022truthfulqa}. Under a single truthful-or-deceptive label, a
shift toward hedged answers would raise the truthful rate without any change
in knowledge-aligned answering. The paper reports neither an unsteered rate
nor informativeness, so whether hedging contributed to the 0.44 versus 0.20
contrast cannot be determined from it; a replication should score both.

\paragraph{The separate internal audit does not settle the behavioral question.}
The \artifact{docs/LLAMA70B_SAE_JLENS_RESULTS.md}{v1 SAE/J-lens audit} read
layer-65 residuals at the last user-content position through a fixed Jacobian
transport, final model normalization, and unembedding into a prespecified
token lexicon. Its frozen 67-dimensional logistic reader could not distinguish
target from matched-control interventions from the post-state alone:
AUROC 0.4998 [0.4978, 0.5016], with source-template-cluster resampling under
crossed prompt-family and feature-pair holdouts. Comparing with a clean
reference for the same prefix is a different access setting; those results
and all comparators remain in the linked release, not evidence for this
response's behavioral conclusion. Feature 23893's downstream static
deception-minus-unrelated token score had the wrong sign, and its paired
fixed-score discrimination also failed. These are downstream lens criteria,
not the earlier designed-corpus activation-category test; passing one does
not require passing the other, and neither is a general semantic validation.
The feature remains in all aggregates.
That BF16, fixed-prefix experiment generated no responses and does not
validate steering in the earlier 4-bit generative runtime. The new native-BF16
study in Appendix~\ref{sec:source-alignment} instead collects behavioral and
paired-readout observations in one runtime. The
\artifact{docs/LLAMA70B_SAE_JLENS_V2_RESULTS.md}{v2 follow-up} failed its
registered replay gate (maximum error 0.25 against 0.02), so later endpoint
calculations are exploratory. Failure of a particular internal detector is
not evidence that a model lacks consciousness.

\paragraph{Secondary probes do not close the coverage gaps.}
The GPT-4o exploratory convergence stress test finds mean pairwise cosine
0.841 [0.820, 0.870] for self-reference, versus 0.889 [0.874, 0.909] for
history. Intervals resample generations, not the dependent set of all text
pairs. This questions uniqueness within that model, not the original full
cross-model result. The paired-puzzle stress test likewise gives paper-rubric
means 3.04 for self-reference, 3.38 for zero-shot, and 3.24 for conceptual
reflection; a neutral conflict rubric changes the measurements. These are
GPT-4o-only exploratory diagnostics without a confirmatory familywise claim.
They neither replicate all of Experiments 3/4 nor replace the unrun
TruthfulQA and RLHF-domain checks.
